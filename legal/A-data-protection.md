# A. Privacy and data protection law in Kenya — research memo for the ResultFormPIIRemover decision

**Verification pass:** 31 October 2026, against primary sources (Kenya Law search API, Parliament, IEBC, Supreme Court sites; ODPC site was under maintenance). Labels below were changed to CONFIRMED / CORRECTED / STILL UNVERIFIED and corrected passages are marked "(corrected, see A-verification.md)". Most important correction: *ODM v IEBC* (2019) ordered the Kibra register published **with** ID/passport numbers; it is not authority for severing identifiers (§1.4, §2.6, §9.2, §9.7).

**As of:** 31 October 2026
**Purpose:** feed the decision on how a tool that redacts personal data from published election result forms (Forms 34A/34B and others) should behave: what must be masked, whether pages may be sent to vision models hosted outside Kenya, whether a DPIA is needed, what to do if a leak is found after publication.

## 1. Decision-relevant findings (summary)

1. **Cross-border transfer / localisation is the hardest problem.** DPA s.25(h), s.48 and s.49 govern any transfer out of Kenya; s.50 lets the Cabinet Secretary prescribe processing that "shall only be effected through a server or a data centre located in Kenya"; **reg. 26 of the General Regulations 2021 does so for processing "for the purpose of … facilitating the conduct of elections for the representation of the people under the Constitution"**: the controller/processor must "(a) process such personal data through a server and data centre located in Kenya; or (b) store at least one serving copy of the concerned personal data in a data centre located in Kenya" (VERIFIED, §3.1). The 2026 ODPC Cross-border Guidance Notes reportedly treat storage on foreign cloud servers as a transfer and require notification to the ODPC (STILL UNVERIFIED as to text — the ODPC website was under maintenance and every PDF returned 404 on 31 Oct 2026; secondary accounts conflict on whether notification is mandatory or "on request"; see A-verification.md A2). Reg. 26 itself, and the absence of any reg. 2 definition of "strategic interest of the state" or "public good", are now CONFIRMED from Kenya Law (A-verification.md A1). Sending **held (unredacted) pages** to Ollama Cloud would move ID numbers, phone numbers and signatures abroad; if signatures are treated as biometric/sensitive data, **s.49(1) requires the data subject's consent** before any processing of sensitive data outside Kenya, which is not obtainable at scale. Sending **redacted** pages (names, party, results visible) is still a transfer of personal data needing a s.48 basis and documentation under reg. 41(2).
2. **Agents' names beside party names.** The s.2 definition of "sensitive personal data" lists "conscience, belief" and "biometric data" but **not** "political opinion" or "political affiliation" (VERIFIED). Party agency is therefore not expressly sensitive data; it is ordinary personal data whose publication on Form 34A is required by law (Elections Act s.39(1C)(c), VERIFIED). A September 2026 ODPC **draft** notice under s.47 proposes adding "political affiliation" as a sensitive category, limited to specified cross-border-transfer circumstances (CONFIRMED as to existence and draft status — ODPC page title indexed; *The Standard*, 1 Oct 2026: comments were open to 25 Sept 2026 and "political affiliation includes information from which political leanings can reasonably be inferred"; the notice text itself is STILL UNVERIFIED and no final/gazetted notice was found; see A-verification.md B4). Keep watching this.
3. **Signatures.** The DPA's "biometric data" definition is non-exhaustive ("including blood typing, fingerprinting, deoxyribonucleic acid analysis, earlobe geometry, retinal scanning and voice recognition"); the Elections (Technology) Regulations 2017 define "biometric" to include "signatures" (both VERIFIED). CMCA s.29 criminalises fraudulent use of another's "electronic signature … or any other unique identification feature". The conservative reading is that a handwritten signature image is at least personal data and arguably biometric/sensitive. Masking the body of each signature is well supported.
4. **ID and phone numbers.** "Identifiable natural person" is defined by reference to "an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier" (VERIFIED); the ATI Act's "personal information" expressly includes "any identifying number" and "telephone or other contact details" (VERIFIED). In *ODM v IEBC* (Kibra, 2019) the High Court did **not** order the register released "minus personal details": it held that IEBC "has not demonstrated how Article 31 of the Constitution of Kenya is" infringed, found that "for free and fair election to be had, national I/D or passport numbers provide" the needed verification, and issued mandamus "compelling the Respondent to publish the Kibra Constituency voters' register containing the names, National ID and Passport numbers and their" polling stations (CORRECTED, see A-verification.md C1; the "minus personal details" reading came from one press report). Reg. 37 of the General Regulations deems a breach involving "the data subject's full name or identification number" together with Second Schedule data to carry a "real risk of harm" (CONFIRMED, §4.1.9). Masking IDs and phone numbers while leaving names therefore rests on the DPA's minimisation and security duties (ss.25(d), 41, 51(1)), ATI s.6(1)(d) and Art. 24(1)(e) — **not** on *ODM v IEBC*, which cuts the other way where a party seeks the register under Art. 35 (corrected, see A-verification.md).
5. **Public-role exception.** There is **no** general "public official" carve-out in the DPA. The closest hooks are s.30(1)(b)(ii) (legal obligation), (iv)–(vi) (public task/official authority), s.45(b) (sensitive data "manifestly made public by the data subject"), s.51(2)(c) (exemption where "disclosure is required by or under any written law"), and ATI Act s.6(1)(d)/(4)/(6) (privacy exemption subject to a public-interest override weighted toward accountability). Case law treats officials' *official* information (e.g. remuneration) as not private but has not held that officials' ID or phone numbers lose protection (§6).
6. **DPIA.** s.31 requires a DPIA before "high risk" processing; reg. 49(1) lists triggers that fit this tool squarely: "(b) use of personal data on a large-scale for a purpose other than that for which the data was initially collected", "(c) processing biometric or genetic data", "(g) large scale processing", "(i) innovative use or application of new technological or organizational solutions" (VERIFIED). s.31(5): reports "shall be submitted sixty days prior to the processing"; reg. 52(3): silence for 60 days = deemed approval. Courts have quashed rollouts for want of a DPIA (*Katiba Institute* 2021; *Worldcoin* 2025).
7. **Breach notification.** s.43: notify the Data Commissioner "without delay, within seventy-two hours of becoming aware", and the data subject "within a reasonably practical period"; processors notify controllers within 48 hours (VERIFIED). Reg. 37(3)(a) excludes "personal data that is publicly available", but 37(4) says data is not publicly available "solely because of any data breach" (VERIFIED). A leaked ID/phone number on a published page is prima facie notifiable.
8. **Exemptions do not switch off minimisation.** s.51(1): even exempt processing must comply with principles on "lawful processing, minimisation of collection, data quality, and adopting security safeguards" (VERIFIED). That is the legal footing for redaction-before-publication even where publication itself is mandatory.
9. **Penalties.** Administrative fine up to KES 5m or 1% of turnover, whichever is lower (s.63); failure to obey an enforcement notice: KES 5m / 2 years (s.58(3)); unlawful disclosure offences (s.72); general penalty KES 3m / 10 years (s.73); civil compensation incl. distress (s.65) (all VERIFIED). ODPC penalties to date: KES 5m (Oppo — CONFIRMED by [2023] KEHC 20418; Whitepath and Regus — CONFIRMED, *The Star* 12 Apr 2023; Regus reduced on appeal to KES 2.5m in [2025] KEHC 13491), KES 4.55m (Roma School), KES 2.975m (Mulla Pride), KES 1.85m (Casa Vera) (CONFIRMED, *The Star*/*The Standard* 26 Sept 2023 quoting the ODPC statement; corrected, see A-verification.md I).
10. **No amendment to the Act was found as at the Sept 2026 Kenya Law capture**: the "Is amended by" list shows only the 24th Annual Supplement (LN 221 of 2023, a revision, not a substantive amendment). A "Data Protection (Amendment) Bill 2025" is **not** before Parliament: it appears in neither the National Assembly Bills Tracker 2026 (as at 15 Oct 2026) nor the National Assembly/Senate bills pages nor Kenya Law's bills collection; secondary sources describe it as a civil-society (DPGSK) draft, and the claim that it "took effect 5 Oct 2026" has no primary support (CORRECTED, see A-verification.md G1).

## 2. The Constitution of Kenya, 2010

Primary: `https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2010/constitution` (text VERIFIED against a Kenya Law-derived mirror).

### 2.1 Article 31 — Privacy (VERIFIED)
> "Every person has the right to privacy, which includes the right not to have— (a) their person, home or property searched; (b) their possessions seized; (c) information relating to their family or private affairs unnecessarily required or revealed; or (d) the privacy of their communications infringed."

Note the qualifier "unnecessarily": the text itself invites a necessity analysis of any revelation of private information.

### 2.2 Article 24 — Limitation of rights (VERIFIED)
> "(1) A right or fundamental freedom in the Bill of Rights shall not be limited except by law, and then only to the extent that the limitation is reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom, taking into account all relevant factors, including— (a) the nature of the right or fundamental freedom; (b) the importance of the purpose of the limitation; (c) the nature and extent of the limitation; (d) the need to ensure that the enjoyment of rights and fundamental freedoms by any individual does not prejudice the rights and fundamental freedoms of others; and (e) the relation between the limitation and its purpose and whether there are less restrictive means to achieve the purpose. (2) … a provision in legislation limiting a right … (c) shall not limit the right or fundamental freedom so far as to derogate from its core or essential content. (3) The State or a person seeking to justify a particular limitation shall demonstrate to the court … that the requirements of this Article have been satisfied."

Art. 24(1)(e) ("less restrictive means") is the constitutional argument for redaction: publishing the form with IDs/phones/signatures masked achieves the transparency purpose with a smaller intrusion than publishing them whole.

### 2.3 Article 35 — Access to information (VERIFIED)
> "(1) Every citizen has the right of access to— (a) information held by the State; and (b) information held by another person and required for the exercise or protection of any right or fundamental freedom. (2) Every person has the right to the correction or deletion of untrue or misleading information that affects the person. (3) The State shall publish and publicise any important information affecting the nation."

### 2.4 Article 10 — National values (VERIFIED)
> "(2) The national values and principles of governance include— … (c) good governance, integrity, transparency and accountability; …" They "bind all State organs, State officers, public officers and all persons whenever any of them— … (b) enacts, applies or interprets any law; or (c) makes or implements public policy decisions" (Art. 10(1)).

### 2.5 Articles 86 and 88 (VERIFIED, context)
Art. 86: at every election the IEBC shall ensure "(a) whatever voting method is used, the system is simple, accurate, verifiable, secure, accountable and transparent; (b) the votes cast are counted, tabulated and the results announced promptly by the presiding officer at each polling station; (c) the results from the polling stations are openly and accurately collated and promptly announced by the returning officer". Art. 88(4) lists IEBC functions. These are the constitutional source of the duty to publish results forms, which is the "legal obligation" for processing agents'/officers' data.

## 3. The Data Protection Act, 2019 (No. 24 of 2019; Cap. 411C)

Assented 8 Nov 2019; commenced 25 Nov 2019. Primary: `https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2019/24/eng@2022-12-31`; original Gazette text also at `https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf`. All quotations in this section are VERIFIED against the Kenya Law consolidated capture.

**Amendments.** The Kenya Law page's "Is amended by" list contains only "24th Annual Supplement (Legal Notice 221 of 2023)" — a revision exercise effective 31 Dec 2022. I found **no substantive amending Act 2020–2026**. A "Data Protection (Amendment) Bill, 2025" (AI governance, cross-border controls, a Data Protection Appeals Tribunal at proposed ss.64A–64F) is described by law-firm blogs as pending in Parliament; one aggregator claims it took effect 5 Oct 2026. **CORRECTED (see A-verification.md G1):** checked on 31 Oct 2026, no such Bill appears in the National Assembly Bills Tracker 2026 ("the status of all Bills before the National Assembly in the year 2026", as at 15 Oct 2026), in the National Assembly or Senate bills lists, in the Parliament library, or in Kenya Law's bills collection (0 hits), and Kenya Law's consolidated DPA is still the `eng@2022-12-31` expression. Secondary sources attribute the text to the Data Privacy and Governance Society of Kenya (a civil-society draft). Treat the Act as unamended.

### 3.1 Application (s.4)
> "This Act applies to the processing of personal data— (a) entered in a record, by or for a data controller or processor, by making use of automated or non-automated means … (b) by a data controller or data processor who— (i) is established or ordinarily resident in Kenya and processes personal data while in Kenya; or (ii) not established or ordinarily resident in Kenya, but processing personal data of data subjects located in Kenya."

A cloud model host abroad processing Kenyans' data falls within (b)(ii) as a processor.

### 3.2 Definitions (s.2)
- **personal data**: "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person".
- **identifiable natural person**: "a person who can be identified directly or indirectly, by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social or social identity". *ID/passport and phone numbers are squarely "identification numbers"/"online identifiers".*
- **sensitive personal data**: "data revealing the natural person's race, health status, ethnic social origin, conscience, belief, genetic data, biometric data, property details, marital status, family details including names of the person's children, parents, spouse or spouses, sex or the sexual orientation of the data subject". *No express mention of political opinion, political affiliation, trade-union membership or signatures.* (A different list sometimes attributed to the DPA — "race, sex, pregnancy, marital status, health status, ethnic social origin, colour, age, disability, religion, conscience, belief…" — is actually the ATI Act's definition of "personal information", §8.1.)
- **biometric data**: "personal data resulting from specific technical processing based on physical, physiological or behavioural characterisation including blood typing, fingerprinting, deoxyribonucleic acid analysis, earlobe geometry, retinal scanning and voice recognition". *Non-exhaustive ("including"); a handwritten signature is a "behavioural characterisation" but is only "biometric data" if it results from "specific technical processing" — an image of a signature on a form is borderline; a signature-recognition model run over it is not.*
- **data controller**: "a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purpose and means of processing of personal data".
- **data processor**: "a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which processes personal data on behalf of the data controller".
- **processing**: "any operation or sets of operations which is performed on personal data … such as (a) collection, recording, organisation, structuring; (b) storage, adaptation or alteration; (c) retrieval, consultation or use; (d) disclosure by transmission, dissemination, or otherwise making available; or (e) alignment or combination, restriction, erasure or destruction." *Publication on a portal is "disclosure by … dissemination"; sending an image to a model is "transmission" and "use".*
- **anonymisation**: "the removal of personal identifiers from personal data so that the data subject is no longer identifiable". *Partial masking of 3 digits is pseudonymisation at best, not anonymisation, since the name remains.*
- **pseudonymisation**: "the processing of personal data in such a manner that the personal data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, and such additional information is kept separately…"
- **personal data breach**: "a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data transmitted, stored or otherwise processed".
- **consent**: "any manifestation of express, unequivocal, free, specific and informed indication of the data subject's wishes by a statement or by a clear affirmative action".

### 3.3 Principles (s.25)
> "Every data controller or data processor shall ensure that personal data is— (a) processed in accordance with the right to privacy of the data subject; (b) processed lawfully, fairly and in a transparent manner …; (c) collected for explicit, specified and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; (d) adequate, relevant, limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed; (e) collected only where a valid explanation is provided whenever information relating to family or private affairs is required; (f) accurate …; (g) kept in a form which identifies the data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes which it was collected; and (h) not transferred outside Kenya, unless there is proof of adequate data protection safeguards or consent from the data subject."

### 3.4 Lawful bases (s.30)
> "(1) A data controller or data processor shall not process personal data, unless— (a) the data subject consents …; or (b) the processing is necessary— (i) for the performance of a contract …; (ii) for compliance with any legal obligation to which the controller is subject; (iii) in order to protect the vital interests …; (iv) for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller; (v) the performance of any task carried out by a public authority; (vi) for the exercise, by any person in the public interest, of any other functions of a public nature; (vii) for the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or data processor by a third party to whom the data is disclosed, except if the processing is unwarranted in any particular case having regard to the harm and prejudice to the rights and freedoms or legitimate interests of the data subject; or (viii) for the purpose of historical, statistical, journalistic, literature and art or scientific research. (2) Further processing of personal data shall be in accordance with the purpose of collection. (3) A data controller who contravenes the provisions of subsection (1) commits an offence."

Reg. 5 of the General Regulations adds that only "one legal basis for processing at a time" may be relied on and it "shall be established before the processing" and be "demonstrable at all times" (VERIFIED, §4.1).

For the IEBC (or a returning officer) publishing forms: (b)(ii) + Elections Act s.39(1C)(c), and (b)(iv)/(v). For a third party (observer group, newsroom) re-publishing redacted forms: (b)(vii) legitimate interests, (b)(viii) journalistic/research, and s.28(2)(a) (indirect collection permitted where "the data is contained in a public record"). Redacting the forms and then **sending them to a vision model** is "further processing" that must be "in accordance with the purpose of collection" (s.30(2)) — defensible as quality-assurance of the publication, weaker for a cloud host abroad.

### 3.5 Data protection impact assessment (s.31)
> "(1) Where a processing operation is likely to result in high risk to the rights and freedoms of a data subject, by virtue of its nature, scope, context and purposes, a data controller or data processor shall, prior to the processing, carry out a data protection impact assessment. (2) A data protection impact assessment shall include— (a) a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes …; (b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality …; (c) an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects; (d) the measures envisaged to address the risks and the safeguards, security measures and mechanisms …. (3) The data controller or data processor shall consult the Data Commissioner prior to the processing if a data protection impact assessment … indicates that the processing of the data would result in a high risk …. (5) The data impact assessment reports shall be submitted sixty days prior to the processing of data. (6) The Data Commissioner shall set out guidelines for carrying out an impact assessment under this section."

Reg. 49 lists the triggers (§4.1.10). Case law: *R v Mucheru ex parte Katiba Institute* [2021] KEHC 122 (Huduma cards quashed as ultra vires s.31); *Republic v Tools for Humanity Corp. & 8 others; Katiba Institute & 4 others (ex parte)* [2025] KEHC 5629 (Worldcoin; failure to conduct a DPIA among the grounds) — §6.

### 3.6 Protection by design and by default (s.41) and organisational measures (s.42)
> s.41 "(1) Every data controller or data processor shall implement appropriate technical and organisational measures which are designed— (a) to implement the data protection principles in an effective manner; and (b) to integrate necessary safeguards for that purpose into the processing. (2) The duty … applies both at the time of the determination of the means of processing the data and at the time of the processing. (3) … for ensuring that, by default, only personal data which is necessary for each specific purpose is processed, taking into consideration— (a) the amount of personal data collected; (b) the extent of its processing; (c) the period of its storage; (d) its accessibility; and (e) the cost of processing data and the technologies and tools used. (4) … measures such as— (a) to identify reasonably foreseeable internal and external risks …; (b) to establish and maintain appropriate safeguards …; (c) to the pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data; (d) … restore the availability and access …; (e) to verify that the safeguards are effectively implemented; and (f) to ensure that the safeguards are continually updated …."

> s.42 "(1) In determining the appropriate measures referred to in section 41, in particular, where the processing involves the transmission of data over an information and communication network, a data controller shall have regard to— (a) the state of technological development available; (b) the cost of implementing any of the security measures; (c) the special risks that exist in the processing of the data; and (d) the nature of the data being processed. (2) Where a data controller is using the services of a data processor— (a) the data controller shall opt for a data processor who provides sufficient guarantees …; and (b) the data controller and the data processor shall enter into a written contract …. (3) Where a data processor processes personal data other than as instructed by the data controller, the data processor shall be deemed to be a data controller in respect of that processing."

*The tool's design (8×8 block masking, leak checks, canary tests, hash-verified crops, "nothing leaves the machine" option) maps directly onto s.41(3)–(4) and s.42(1). s.42(2)(b) requires a written processor contract with any cloud model host.*

### 3.7 Data subjects' rights (ss.26, 29, 35–40) and their limits for records published under a legal obligation
- s.26: rights "(a) to be informed of the use to which their personal data is to be put; (b) to access …; (c) to object to the processing of all or part of their personal data; (d) to correction of false or misleading data; and (e) to deletion of false or misleading data about them."
- s.29 (duty to notify before collection) includes "(d) the third parties whose personal data has been or will be transferred to, including details of safeguards adopted" and "(g) the data being collected pursuant to any law and whether such collection is voluntary or mandatory".
- s.36 (objection): "A data subject has a right to object to the processing of their personal data, unless the data controller or data processor demonstrates compelling legitimate interest for the processing which overrides the data subject's interests, or for the establishment, exercise or defence of a legal claim." Reg. 8(3): comply within 14 days; reg. 8(4): objection is absolute only for direct marketing.
- s.35 (automated decisions): right not to be subject to a decision "based solely on automated processing … which produces legal effects concerning or significantly affects the data subject" — a VLM deciding to mask/withhold a page is not such a decision about the data subject, but a wholly automated decision to *publish* a person's unredacted identifiers could be argued to "significantly affect" them; keep a human-review path.
- s.40 (rectification and erasure): request to "erase or destroy without undue delay personal data that the data controller or data processor is no longer authorised to retain, irrelevant, excessive or obtained unlawfully"; s.40(3): where data "is required for the purposes of evidence", restrict processing instead of erasing. **Reg. 12(4)**: "A right of erasure does not apply if processing is necessary … (a) to exercise the right of freedom of expression and information; (b) to comply with a legal obligation; (c) for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority; (d) for archiving purposes in the public interest …; or (e) for the establishment, exercise or defence of a legal claim." *An agent cannot force erasure of a lawfully published form; they can object to publication of surplus identifiers, which is what redaction pre-empts.*
- s.37 (commercial use): no commercial use of personal data without express consent or statutory authority; anonymise "where possible".

### 3.8 Retention (s.39)
> "(1) A data controller or data processor shall retain personal data only as long as may be reasonably necessary to satisfy the purpose for which it is processed unless the retention is— (a) required or authorised by law; (b) reasonably necessary for a lawful purpose; (c) authorised or consented by the data subject; or (d) for historical, statistical, journalistic literature and art or research purposes. (2) A data controller or data processor shall delete, erase, anonymise or pseudonymise personal data not necessary to be retained under subsection (1) …."
Reg. 19 requires a written retention schedule with periodic audits (§4.1.6). *Relevant to the originals, held pages, review folders and VLM crops.*

### 3.9 Breach notification (s.43)
> "(1) Where personal data has been accessed or acquired by an unauthorised person, and there is a real risk of harm to the data subject …, a data controller shall— (a) notify the Data Commissioner without delay, within seventy-two hours of becoming aware of such breach; and (b) subject to subsection (3), communicate to the data subject in writing within a reasonably practical period, unless the identity of the data subject cannot be established. (2) Where the notification … is not made within seventy-two hours, the notification shall be accompanied by reasons for the delay. (3) Where a data processor becomes aware of a personal data breach, the data processor shall notify the data controller without delay and where reasonably practicable, within forty-eight hours …. (5) The notification … shall provide … (a) description of the nature of the data breach; (b) … measures … taken; (c) recommendation on the measures to be taken by the data subject …; (d) where applicable, the identity of the unauthorised person …; (e) the name and contact details of the data protection officer …. (6) The communication of a breach to the data subject shall not be required where the data controller or data processor has implemented appropriate security safeguards which may include encryption of affected personal data. … (8) The data controller shall record … (a) the facts relating to the breach; (b) its effects; and (c) the remedial action taken."

### 3.10 Sensitive personal data (ss.44–47)
- s.44: "No category of sensitive personal data shall be processed unless section 25 applies to that processing."
- s.45: permitted grounds — "(a) … legitimate activities with appropriate safeguards by a foundation, association or any other not-for profit body with a political, philosophical, religious or trade union aim and on condition that— (i) the processing relates solely to the members …; and (ii) the personal data is not disclosed outside that body without the consent of the data subject. (b) the processing relates to personal data which is manifestly made public by the data subject; or (c) processing is necessary for— (i) the establishment, exercise or defence of a legal claim; (ii) the purpose of carrying out the obligations and exercising specific rights of the controller or of the data subject; or (iii) protecting the vital interests …." *Note: neither consent nor "legal obligation" of a public body appears as a stand-alone ground; (c)(ii) is the nearest fit for IEBC.*
- s.46: health data.
- s.47: "(1) The Data Commissioner may prescribe further categories of personal data which may be classified as sensitive personal data." (Basis for the Sept 2026 draft notice on "political affiliation" (and, per secondary sources, "trade union membership"): existence and consultation to 25 Sept 2026 CONFIRMED via *The Standard* 1 Oct 2026 and the ODPC page title; text STILL UNVERIFIED and no final notice found — see A-verification.md B4.)

### 3.11 Transfers outside Kenya (ss.48–50)
> s.48 "A data controller or data processor may transfer personal data to another country only where— (a) the data controller or data processor has given proof to the Data Commissioner on the appropriate safeguards with respect to the security and protection of the personal data; (b) … proof to the Data Commissioner of the appropriate safeguards …, and the appropriate safeguards including jurisdictions with commensurate data protection laws; (c) the transfer is necessary— (i) for the performance of a contract …; (ii) …; (iii) for any matter of public interest; (iv) for the establishment, exercise or defence of a legal claim; (v) … vital interests …; or (vi) for the purpose of compelling legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or data processor which are not overridden by the interests, rights and freedoms of the data subjects."

> s.49 "(1) The processing of sensitive personal data out of Kenya shall only be effected upon obtaining consent of a data subject and on obtaining confirmation of appropriate safeguards. (2) The Data Commissioner may request a person who transfers data to another country to demonstrate the effectiveness of the security safeguards or the existence of compelling legitimate interests. (3) The Data Commissioner may, in order to protect the rights and fundamental freedoms of data subjects, prohibit, suspend or subject the transfer to such conditions as may be determined."

> s.50 "The Cabinet Secretary may prescribe, based on grounds of strategic interests of the state or protection of revenue, certain nature of processing that shall only be effected through a server or a data centre located in Kenya."

s.71(2)(g) confirms the regulation-making power for "the processing of data through a data server or data centre in Kenya". The prescription was made in reg. 26 (§4.1.8).

### 3.12 Exemptions (ss.51–55)
> s.51 "(1) Nothing in this Part shall exempt any data controller or data processor from complying with data protection principles relating to lawful processing, minimisation of collection, data quality, and adopting security safeguards to protect personal data. (2) The processing of personal data is exempt from the provisions of this Act if— (a) it relates to processing of personal data by an individual in the course of a purely personal or household activity; (b) if it is necessary for national security or public interest; or (c) disclosure is required by or under any written law or by an order of the court."

> s.52 "(1) The principles of processing personal data shall not apply where— (a) processing is undertaken by a person for the publication of a literary or artistic material; (b) data controller reasonably believes that publication would be in the public interest; and (c) data controller reasonably believes that, in all the circumstances, compliance with the provision is incompatible with the special purposes. (2) Subsection (1)(b) shall only apply where it can be demonstrated that the processing is in compliance with any self-regulatory or issued code of ethics …. (3) The Data Commissioner shall prepare a code of practice … for purposes of Journalism, Literature and Art."

> s.53 research/history/statistics: further processing compatible if "solely for such purposes and will not be published in an identifiable form"; exempt only if "results of the research or resulting statistics are not made available in a form which identifies the data subject".

> s.54 "The Data Commissioner may prescribe other instances where compliance with certain provisions of this Act may be exempted." s.55: data-sharing code for exchange "between government departments or public sector agencies".

Regs. 54–57 narrow s.51(2)(b): "national security" means processing by Art. 239 national security organs, with a Cabinet Secretary exemption certificate; "public interest" means a "permitted general situation" (serious threat to life/health/safety; suspected unlawful activity or serious misconduct; missing person; legal claim; ADR; diplomatic duties) or a "permitted health situation" (§4.1.11). **There is no electoral-body exemption.** The IEBC's route is s.51(2)(c) ("disclosure is required by … written law") — which still leaves s.51(1) minimisation and security in force.

### 3.13 Enforcement, offences and penalties (ss.56–66, 72–74)
- s.56(5): complaints "shall be investigated and concluded within ninety days".
- s.58: enforcement notice; non-compliance "without reasonable excuse" — "a fine not exceeding five million shillings or to imprisonment for a term not exceeding two years, or to both" (s.58(3)).
- s.62: penalty notice; factors include "(c) any action taken … to mitigate the damage or distress", "(g) the categories of personal data affected", "(h) … whether … the data controller or data processor notified the Data Commissioner of the failure", "(j) adherence to approved codes of conduct or certification mechanisms".
- s.63: "the maximum amount of the penalty … is up to five million shillings, or in the case of an undertaking, up to one per centum of its annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is lower."
- s.64: appeal to the High Court. s.65: compensation for "damage", which "includes financial loss and damage not involving financial loss, including distress"; processor liable only if it breached a processor-specific duty or acted "outside, or contrary to, the data controller's lawful instructions".
- s.72: offences of unlawful disclosure — controller disclosing "in any manner that is incompatible with the purpose for which such data has been collected"; processor disclosing "without the prior authority of the data controller"; any person who "(a) obtains access to personal data … without prior authority …; or (b) discloses personal data to third party"; offering such data for sale.
- s.73: "a fine not exceeding three million shillings or to an imprisonment term not exceeding ten years, or to both"; court may order forfeiture of equipment.
- s.74: Data Commissioner may issue guidelines, codes, certification and "sector specific guidelines".

## 4. The 2021 Regulations (and later instruments)

### 4.1 Data Protection (General) Regulations, 2021 — LN 263 of 2021
Gazetted 31 Dec 2021 (Kenya Gazette Supplement No. 236); Kenya Law lists commencement 14 Jan 2022. Primary: `https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/ln/2021/263/eng@2022-12-31`; ODPC copy `https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/THE-DATA-PROTECTION-GENERAL-REGULATIONS-2021-1.pdf`. All quotations VERIFIED against the Gazette text unless noted.

1. **Reg. 3 (scope):** "These Regulations shall not apply to civil registration entities specified under the Data Protection (Civil Registration) Regulations, 2020" (LN 196 of 2020).
2. **Reg. 4 (consent):** controller must inform the data subject of, inter alia, "(e) the possible risks of data transfers due to absence of an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards".
3. **Reg. 5 (lawful basis):** "(1) A data controller or data processor may process data without consent of a data subject if the processing is necessary for any reason set out in section 30(1)(b) of the Act. (2) Processing under sub-regulation (1) shall only rely on one legal basis for processing at a time, which shall be established before the processing. (3) The legal basis relied on … shall be demonstrable at all times …."
4. **Reg. 6 (indirect collection)** permits collection from "(b) publications or databases" and "(e) biometric technology, including voice or facial recognition", subject to minimisation.
5. **Reg. 8 (objection)** and **reg. 12 (erasure)** — see §3.7. Reg. 12(1)(f): erasure may be requested where "the erasure is necessary to comply with a legal obligation".
6. **Reg. 19 (retention):** "(2) A data controller or data processor shall— (a) establish personal data retention schedule with appropriate time limits for the periodic review …; and (b) erase, delete anonymise or pseudonymise personal data upon the lapse of the purpose …. (3) A personal data retention schedule … shall outline the— (a) purpose for retention; (b) the retention period; (c) provision for periodic audit …; and (d) actions to be taken after the audit …." Reg. 35 (storage-limitation elements) adds "(d) ensuring that it is not possible to re-identify anonymised data or recover deleted data and testing whether this is possible" and "(f) determining which personal data and length of storage is necessary for back-ups and logs".
7. **Regs. 21, 23, 24, 25:** data sharing requires agreements for routine sharing (21(4)); a published data protection policy must cover "(e) obligations or requirements where personal data is to be transferred outside the country, to third parties, or other data controllers or data processors located outside Kenya and where possible, specify such recipients" (23(2)(e)); the controller–processor contract must contain processing details, instructions, confidentiality commitments, security measures, "(e) provision stipulating that all personal data must be permanently deleted or returned on termination", and audit rights (24(2)); "(1) A data processor shall not engage the services of a third party without the prior authorisation of the data controller" (25(1)).
8. **Reg. 26 — "Requirement for specified processing to be done in Kenya" (the localisation rule; heading corrected, see A-verification.md A1 — Kenya Law's text also reads "subregulation", not "sub-regulation"):**
   > "26. (1) Pursuant to section 50 of the Act, a data controller or data processor who processes personal data for the purpose of strategic interest of the state outlined under sub-regulation (2) shall— (a) process such personal data through a server and data centre located in Kenya; or (b) store at least one serving copy of the concerned personal data in a data centre located in Kenya.
   > (2) The purpose contemplated under sub-regulation (1) includes the processing of personal data for the purpose of— (a) administering of the civil registration and legal identity management systems; **(b) facilitating the conduct of elections for the representation of the people under the Constitution;** (c) overseeing any system for administering public finances by any state organ; (d) running any system designated as a protected computer system in terms of section 20 of the Computer Misuse and Cybercrime Act, 2018; (e) offering any form of early childhood education and basic education under the Basic Education Act, 2013; or (f) provision of primary or secondary health care for a data subject in the country.
   > (3) Despite (2), the Cabinet Secretary may require a data controller who processes personal data outside Kenya to comply with sub-regulation (1), where the data controller— (a) has been notified that personal data outside Kenya has been breached or its services have been used to violate the Act and has not taken measures to stop or handle the violation; and (b) resists, obstructs or fails to comply with requests of the Data Commissioner or any other relevant authority in— (i) cooperating to investigate and handle such violations; or (ii) neutralising and disabling the effect of cyber security protection measures."

   Observations: (i) the phrase the brief asked about — "administering of the electoral process" — does **not** appear; the electoral item is "(b) facilitating the conduct of elections for the representation of the people under the Constitution". (ii) The regulation does not use the phrase "actualising a public good". CONFIRMED (corrected, see A-verification.md A1): Kenya Law's full-text index shows "strategic interest" occurring in LN 263 only in Part IV (reg. 26) and "public good" not at all, so **neither term is defined in reg. 2**; reg. 26(1) is the only place the phrase appears ("for the purpose of strategic interest of the state outlined under subregulation (2)"). DPA s.50 itself uses the plural "strategic interests of the state or protection of revenue". (iii) The list is introduced by "includes", so it is not closed. (iv) The rule is satisfied by **either** in-Kenya processing **or** a serving copy in Kenya; it does not, on its face, prohibit a transient copy being processed abroad if a serving copy is kept in Kenya — but ss.48–49 still apply to the transfer itself.
9. **Regs. 37–38 (breaches):** reg. 37(1) deems a breach to carry "real risk of harm" if it relates to "(a) [the data subject's full name or] identification number and any of the personal data or classes of personal data … set out in the Second Schedule; or (b) [the data subject's] account with a data controller or data processor … (i) the data subject['s account] name or number; and (ii) any password, security code, access code, response to a security question, biometric data or other data that is used or required to allow access …" (bracketed words were my reconstruction; CONFIRMED against Kenya Law snippets, which read "(a) the data subject's full name … or identification number and any of the personal data or classes of personal [data]" and "…password, security code, access code, response to a security question, biometric data or…"; heading "Categories of notifiable data breach" — corrected, see A-verification.md E1). "(3) The personal data … set out in the Second Schedule excludes— (a) any personal data that is publicly available; or (b) any personal data that is disclosed to the extent that is required or permitted under any written law. (4) The personal data referred to in sub-paragraph (3)(a) shall not be publicly available solely because of any data breach." The Second Schedule (r.37) lists remuneration, income, card numbers, bank account numbers, child-protection identifiers, "any private key … used … to create a secure electronic record or secure electronic signature", net worth, deposits/withdrawals, etc. Reg. 38 lists the contents of the notification to the Commissioner (date/circumstances of awareness, chronology, how it occurred, number of data subjects, classes of data, potential harm, remedial actions, communication to affected persons, contact details).
10. **Regs. 39–48 (transfers):** reg. 40: before transfer "ascertain that the transfer is based on— (a) appropriate data protection safeguards; (b) an adequacy decision made by the Data Commissioner; (c) transfer as a necessity; or (d) consent of the data subject." Reg. 41(1): safeguards exist where "(a) a legal instrument containing appropriate safeguards … binding the intended recipient that is essentially equivalent to the protection under the Act" or "(b) the data controller, having assessed all the circumstances …, concludes that appropriate safeguards exist"; reg. 41(2): "the transfer shall be documented … the documentation shall include— (i) the date and time of the transfer; (ii) the name of the recipient; (iii) the justification for the transfer; and (iv) a description of the personal data transferred." Reg. 42 deems safeguards where the destination "(a) ratified the African Union Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection; (b) a reciprocal data protection agreement with Kenya; or (c) a contractual binding corporate rules among a concerned group". Reg. 45: necessity transfers must be "strictly necessary in a specific case outlined under section 48(c)" with no overriding rights. Reg. 46: consent-based transfer requires explicit consent after being "informed of the possible risks"; "(2) … a data controller or processor must seek consent from a data subject for the transfer of sensitive personal data, in accordance with section 49".
11. **Regs. 49–53 (DPIA):**
    > "49. (1) For the purpose of section 31(1) of the Act, processing operations considered to result in high risks to the rights and freedoms of a data subject include— (a) automated decision making with legal or similar significant effect …; (b) use of personal data on a large-scale for a purpose other than that for which the data was initially collected; (c) processing biometric or genetic data; (d) where there is a change in any aspect of the processing that may result in higher risk to data subjects; (e) processing sensitive personal data or data relating to children or vulnerable groups; (f) combining, linking or cross-referencing separate datasets …; (g) large scale processing of personal data; (h) a systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area on a large scale; (i) innovative use or application of new technological or organizational solutions; or (j) where the processing prevents a data subject from exercising a right. (2) A data processor or data controller shall, prior to processing data under sub-regulation (1) conduct a data protection impact assessment."

## 5. ODPC guidance

### 5.1 Guidance Note on Processing Personal Data for Electoral Purposes
- **Document:** "Guidance Notes for Electoral Purposes", `https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ODPC-Guidance-Notes-for-Electoral-Purposes.pdf` (file uploaded Feb 2024; the note itself is dated by most sources to **November 2022**, the Carter Center/Privacy International report says November 2021, and the ODPC listed it among guidance notes announced January 2024 — date **UNVERIFIED**). I could **not** open the PDF in this session, nor in the 31 Oct 2026 verification pass (ODPC website under maintenance; the PDF URL returned 404; no copy found on KICTANet, Katiba Institute, KHRC or the news sites); everything below is from secondary summaries (Business Daily column; Vellum; Carter Center/PI final report; KICTANet; Mzalendo) and is **STILL UNVERIFIED as to wording** (A-verification.md H).
- **Stated basis and scope:** applies to "all stakeholders in the electoral process" — IEBC, Office of the Registrar of Political Parties, political parties, candidates, observers, civil-society registration groups, and their processors; notes that the Elections Act, IEBC Act and Political Parties Act "permit the processing of personal data for electoral purposes"; described as non-binding and as largely restating the Act.
- **Lawful basis:** IEBC's register processing rests on its statutory mandate/public task, not consent; parties' statutory membership registers rest on the Political Parties Act; anything beyond the statutory data needs its own basis (often consent), with the burden of proving consent on the controller (s.32(1)).
- **Objection:** voters may object to processing of some register fields (reported examples: email, phone number) but not name or ID number; objection to political marketing/profiling is highlighted; targeted political advertising should be transparent to the voter.
- **DPIA:** controllers told to "Undertake a Data Protection Impact Assessment (see Guidance Note on DPIAs on www.odpc.go.ke)".
- **Security/retention:** general restatement — keep data only as long as necessary; confidentiality agreements, access controls and staff/volunteer privacy training "as standard practice"; breach-response procedures; third-party processors held to the same standards. No specific retention periods reported.
- **Registration:** entities "canvassing political support" must register with the ODPC (echoing Third Schedule item 1).
- **Result forms / agents / polling officials:** **no source reports any passage on Forms 34A/34B, result forms, agents' or presiding officers' details, or publication of result forms.** Searches for ODPC complaints or statements about agents' data on published forms returned nothing. Treat this as a gap: the Guidance Note appears to address voter and party-member data, not the personal data of election officials and agents printed on results forms.
- **2026 context:** CONFIRMED from the National Assembly Hansard of 15 Oct 2026 (corrected, see A-verification.md F2): the House adopted the Constitutional Oversight Committee's Fourth Report on the IEBC and the ODPC, including recommendations "5. All registered political parties that have not registered with the ODPC to complete registration. IEBC to expedite its registration and full compliance, including data processing and sharing agreements, before the 2027 General Election. 6. The ODPC to update its Guidance Note on Processing of Personal Data for Electoral Purposes within six months to address emerging technologies, and to invest in digital forensic and real-time monitoring tools." The Committee also recorded a "Forty-five per cent staffing shortfall" at the ODPC and the "Absence of Mutual Legal Assistance mechanisms for cross-border enforcement". Press in August 2026 reported renewed unsolicited political SMS ahead of 2027 (*The Star*, 18 Aug 2026, "Political SMS raise questions over Kenyans' personal data" — seen as a search snippet only).

## 9. Application to the ResultFormPIIRemover design

### 9.1 Who is the controller, and what is the lawful basis?
- If run by/for the IEBC: controller = IEBC; basis s.30(1)(b)(ii) (Elections Act s.39(1C)(c)) and (iv)/(v); the processing is "for the purpose of … facilitating the conduct of elections" (reg. 26(2)(b)). Any cloud model host is a **processor** (s.2; s.42(2)(b) contract; reg. 24; reg. 25 sub-processor authorisation).
- If run by a third party on IEBC-published forms: controller = that party; basis s.30(1)(b)(vii)/(viii); collection from a "public record" permitted (s.28(2)(a)); s.52 may disapply the principles for journalistic publication only if a code of ethics is followed (s.52(2)). Reg. 26 may still bite if the processing is characterised as facilitating elections — a cautious operator should assume it does.

### 9.2 What must be masked
- **Signatures:** mask. Personal data; arguably biometric (DPA definition non-exhaustive; Elections (Technology) Regs call signatures biometric); misuse criminalised by CMCA s.29. The band-through-the-middle approach preserves the evidential fact of signing (Art. 86 transparency) while removing the reproducible stroke pattern — a good Art. 24(1)(e) "less restrictive means" story.
- **ID/passport numbers:** mask. Expressly an "identification number" identifier; reg. 37 treats breaches involving ID numbers as carrying real risk of harm (CONFIRMED). *ODM v IEBC* does **not** support this: it ordered a voters' register published with ID/passport numbers because IEBC had not shown how Art. 31 was engaged and the numbers were needed to verify voters (corrected, see A-verification.md C1). The case for masking must therefore be made under the DPA (ss.25(d), 41(3), 51(1)), ATI s.6(1)(d) and Art. 24(1)(e), with the Art. 86 purpose served by names, party and signatures-as-fact rather than by identifiers; and the operator should expect that a party or candidate requesting the unredacted form under Art. 35 may succeed, as in *Kibra*. Partial masking (3 of 8 digits) leaves ~10^3 candidates per name; combined with name and polling station, re-identification risk remains — the `--full-ids` mode is the safer default for the agents' table; for officers (whose names are public anyway) partial masking still reduces fraud risk (SIM-swap, loan-app KYC).
- **Phone numbers:** mask. KICA s.27A ties numbers to real identity; *Odhiambo* (2026) treats numbers as digital identity; CMCA s.27 harassment risk. Keeping the network prefix (`0734…`) is low-risk; hiding 3 of the last 6 leaves 1,000 candidates — acceptable against casual misuse, not against a determined adversary; consider `--full-phones` for agents.
- **Names, party, results, stamps:** leave visible. Names and party of agents/officers are required by the prescribed form, serve Art. 86 verifiability, and are not expressly sensitive data. Flag for review if the s.47 draft notice on "political affiliation" is finalised in a form that is not limited to inbound cross-border data (draft CONFIRMED to exist, consultation closed 25 Sept 2026, text STILL UNVERIFIED — A-verification.md B4). Note that party agency is printed on a form the law requires to be published (reg. 79, Form 34A — CONFIRMED, §8.5).
- **Writing under stamps:** the default (never mask a stamp) can leave a readable ID under a stamp; `--mask-stamps` is the minimisation-compliant default where the ID is legible. Record the choice in the DPIA.

### 9.3 Cross-border processing (Ollama Cloud) — the critical constraint
1. Any upload of a page bearing personal data to servers outside Kenya is a **transfer** (s.25(h); the 2026 Guidance reportedly treats cloud storage abroad as transfer — STILL UNVERIFIED as to text). It needs a s.48 basis — realistically (c)(iii) "any matter of public interest" or (c)(vi) "compelling legitimate interests" — plus documentation under reg. 41(2) (date/time, recipient, justification, description) and, under the 2026 Guidance, notification to the ODPC (STILL UNVERIFIED; secondary accounts differ on whether notification is per transfer or on request — A-verification.md A2). *Worldcoin* (CONFIRMED) shows the High Court treating s.48 as prohibiting transfers without the required safeguards.
2. **Redacted pages** carry names + party + stamps + partially masked numbers: personal data, probably not sensitive. Transfer is arguable under s.48(c), but reg. 26 (CONFIRMED text) requires that the processing for elections be done "through a server and data centre located in Kenya" **or** that "at least one serving copy" be kept "in a data centre located in Kenya" — the tool's local pipeline satisfies the serving-copy limb on the regulation's face; the ODPC's 2026 guidance is reported to read reg. 26 strictly for election data ("a serving copy in Dublin is not enough" — commentary, STILL UNVERIFIED). Parliament has meanwhile **declined** to add a statutory server-location rule to the Elections Act (Kaluma amendment, 22 Oct 2026 — CONFIRMED, §4.4), so reg. 26 remains the only localisation rule for election data.
3. **Held (unredacted) pages** carry ID numbers, phone numbers and signatures. If signatures are biometric → sensitive → **s.49(1): consent of each data subject plus confirmation of safeguards** before processing outside Kenya. Consent from thousands of agents is not feasible; s.45 offers no public-task ground for sensitive data. **Recommendation: do not send held pages to a foreign-hosted model.** Use the local Ollama path (`--vlm-host localhost`) or a Kenya-hosted inference endpoint for `held_pages`, and keep the `--allow-remote-unredacted` gate off by policy. The `--orient` and `--vlm-audit` paths (redacted pages only) are lower risk but still transfers; prefer Kenya-hosted or local models where available.
4. Contract: s.42(2)(b)/reg. 24 written processor terms (instructions, confidentiality, security, deletion/return on termination, audit); reg. 25 prior authorisation for sub-processors (Ollama Cloud's own hosting). Check whether the provider's jurisdiction has ratified the Malabo Convention or has a reciprocal agreement (reg. 42) — the US has not ratified Malabo.
5. If the IEBC's results system is or becomes gazetted CII, reg. 28 of LN 44 of 2024 adds a committee-approval step for any offshore location of "critical information".

### 9.4 DPIA
Mandatory on the tool's own facts (reg. 49(1)(b), (c), (e), (g), (i)); submit to the ODPC ≥ 60 days before processing (s.31(5)); deemed approval after 60 days' silence (reg. 52(3)); consult if residual high risk (s.31(3)); may publish the report (reg. 52(4)). The DPIA should cover: purpose and legal basis; the masking policy per field; residual re-identification risk of partial masking; the VLM review (what leaves the machine, where it goes, hashes/canary tests); retention of originals, held pages and crops (reg. 19 schedule; reg. 35(f) back-ups and logs); breach-response plan; and the s.35 human-review path. *Katiba Institute* (2021) and *Worldcoin* (2025) show courts will quash processing started without a DPIA.

### 9.5 Breach handling after publication
- A leak (e.g. an unmasked signature or ID found on a published page) is "unauthorised disclosure … of personal data" (s.2) and, for ID numbers, deemed to carry "real risk of harm" (reg. 37(1)(a), subject to the garbled text). Reg. 37(3)(a)'s "publicly available" exclusion is weak here: 37(4) says data is not publicly available "solely because of any data breach", and the IEBC's own publication of the original is the very disclosure the redaction exists to narrow.
- Clock: notify the Data Commissioner within **72 hours** of awareness (s.43(1)(a)) with reasons for any delay (s.43(2)); processors (e.g. a reviewer/host) notify the controller within **48 hours** (s.43(3)); inform affected data subjects "within a reasonably practical period" unless identity cannot be established (s.43(1)(b)); content per s.43(5) and reg. 38; record facts/effects/remedy (s.43(8)). Mitigation and self-notification are penalty-reducing factors (s.62(2)(c), (h)).
- Operationally: the hash-chained ledger and manifests give the chronology reg. 38(1)(b) asks for; add a spot-mask/republish step and a notification template.

### 9.6 Retention
Originals and held pages: keep only as long as needed for verification, under a written schedule (reg. 19); the manifests/ledger contain no PII and may be kept indefinitely; VLM crops and prompts should be deleted on completion (reg. 24(2)(e) for processors). Apply reg. 35(d): test that masked blocks cannot be recovered (the coefficient-level JPEG edit should be documented as irreversible).

### 9.7 Public-role argument
Officers' and agents' **names and roles** are official information on a public record, and the public-interest factors in ATI s.6(6)(a), (c), (e) favour disclosure; their **identifiers** are not needed for accountability, so s.6(1)(d) ("unwarranted invasion of the privacy") and DPA s.25(d) favour masking. This is **not** the line the High Court drew in *ODM v IEBC* — there the court found ID numbers necessary to the requester's electoral purpose and ordered them disclosed (corrected, see A-verification.md C1); the distinction to draw is that a published result form serves public verification of the *count*, for which agents' and officers' identifiers add nothing, whereas a party checking a *register* needed ID numbers to identify voters. No Kenyan authority treats officials' ID numbers, phone numbers or signatures as unprotected; equally, no Kenyan authority yet endorses masking them on a published electoral record.
