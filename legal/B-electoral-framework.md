### 0.3 Executive summary of the decision-relevant findings

1. **The publication duty is statutory, narrow in wording, and attaches to the IEBC.** Elections Act s.39(1C)(c) (inserted by Act No. 36 of 2016) requires the Commission, for a presidential election, to "publish the polling result forms on an online public portal maintained by the Commission". Nothing in the Act or Regulations I could find defines what a published "form" must show, requires unredacted publication, or regulates republication by third parties. [PRIMARY: Rev. 2019 edition of the Act and the gazetted 2016 amendment Act, both read in full; see B-verification.md §2.1]
2. **The forms are completed and authenticated at the polling station.** Reg. 79 (LN 128/2012 as amended by LN 72/2017): presiding officer, candidates or agents sign; agents who refuse must record reasons; a copy goes to each party/candidate/agent; a copy is affixed at the public entrance. Refusal or absence of agents does not by itself invalidate the result. [PRIMARY for both the 2012 text and the 2017 amendments: gazetted LN 72/2017 r. 34 and the Rev. 2017 consolidation read in full]
3. **The 2012 prescribed Form 34 carried only names, signatures and dates of the presiding officer, deputy and agents, plus "reasons for refusal to sign".** ID numbers and phone numbers of agents are not required by any regulation I could verify; they are columns on the 2017/2022 Schedule forms (which I could not read) and on the printed 2022 forms. [PRIMARY for 2012 Schedule; UNVERIFIED for 2017/2022 Schedule]
   **(corrected, see B-verification.md §1.1):** the 2017/2022 Schedule has now been read. The Schedule substituted by the **Elections (General) (Amendment) Regulations, 2017 (Legal Notice No. 72 of 2017), reg. 44**, prescribes for Form 34A an "Agents or Candidates (if present)" table with the columns "No. | Name of Candidate or Agent | **ID/Passport No.** | Party Name/Independent Candidate | **Tel. Contact** | Signature | Date"; Form 34B and Form 34C carry the same agents' columns and, in addition, the constituency returning officer's and the Commission chairperson's "ID Number". So the agents' ID/passport and telephone columns **are prescribed by regulation**, not an IEBC design choice; the 2022 printed form reproduces that layout (with 11 rows, a QR code and a serial number, none of which the Schedule prescribes). No prescribed Form 34A/34B field exists for a stamp. [PRIMARY]
4. **Courts treat signatures, stamps and security features as indicators of authenticity, not as independently decisive.** In 2017 the Supreme Court majority relied on a Registrar's scrutiny that counted unstamped, unsigned, photocopied and feature-less Forms 34A/34B as part of a finding that transmission irregularities impugned the integrity of the election; the dissent disputed the counts. In 2022 the Court compared portal images with originals and found them identical, listed the forms' security features (UV ink, micro-text, tapered serialisation, anti-copy, watermark, digital signatures) and rejected tampering claims. Lower courts hold that a missing agent signature (reg. 79(6)) or a missing stamp is not by itself fatal. [EXTRACT/SECONDARY]
   **(verification note, see B-verification.md §3):** the 2017 findings are now [PRIMARY] (Supreme Court's own judgment PDF; figures corrected in §5.3 below); the 2022 portal-against-originals finding and the KIEMS JPEG-to-PDF explanation are [PRIMARY] (5 September 2022 judgment, para 20); the list of 2022 security features comes from the 26 September 2022 reasoned judgment, which could not be obtained, so it stays [EXTRACT]; the lower-court authorities on missing signatures and stamps remain UNVERIFIED (Kenya Law unreachable).
5. **Verifiability in Kenyan law means the ability to trace a declared result back to the polling-station record.** Article 86 (Constitution), *Maina Kiai* (CA 2017: "the polling station is the true locus for the free exercise of the voters' will"), the 2017 and 2022 presidential petitions and the 2026 Omtatah judgment all centre on reconciling Forms 34A against 34B/34C. None turns on reading a signature's strokes or an agent's ID or telephone number.
6. **The IEBC need not keep the portal open indefinitely.** *KHRC & another v Chebukati & 2 others* [2025] KEHC 8 (Petition E400 of 2022, 9 January 2025): closing the portal 90 days after the 2022 election was lawful; the portal is a transmission-period transparency measure; anyone who wants the forms afterwards may apply under the Access to Information Act. Physical records must be kept three years (reg. 93) and electronic data three years (Technology Regs reg. 17), inspectable on High Court authority. [EXTRACT/SECONDARY]
   **(verification note, see B-verification.md §7, §4, §5):** the holding and the judge (Mugambi J) are confirmed from The Standard's report of 21 January 2025 [SECONDARY]; the neutral citation and petition number could not be re-checked. Reg. 93 and Technology Regs reg. 17 are now [PRIMARY]. **(corrected, see B-verification.md §6.3):** the portal was closed after 90 days in 2022, but `forms.iebc.or.ke` is **online again on 31 October 2026 and serves the 2022 General Election forms** (all 47 counties plus Diaspora, drill-down to each polling station, per-form PDF downloads) as well as the 2022–2026 by-elections.
7. **No evidence that the IEBC ever redacted anything on published forms in 2013, 2017 or 2022, and no reported ODPC or civil-society complaint about agents' or officers' ID numbers, phone numbers or signatures being visible on the portal.** Civil society (KHRC's Kura Yangu Sauti Yangu) complained in 2022 about the opposite problem: illegible forms and forms "neither signed nor stamped". [SECONDARY]
   **(corrected, see B-verification.md §6.3–6.4):** "no redaction" is now confirmed by direct inspection [PRIMARY]: a presidential Form 34A downloaded from the live IEBC portal on 31 October 2026 shows the complete photographed form, including the agents' handwritten names, eight-digit ID numbers, ten-digit telephone numbers and signatures, the presiding and deputy presiding officers' names and signatures, and the "I.E.B.C. PRESIDING OFFICER" stamps, with nothing blurred. The Kura Yangu Sauti Yangu briefing quoted here is dated **10 August 2017** and concerns the 2017 election, not 2022. The absence of ODPC or civil-society complaints about identifiers on the portal remains a negative finding from searching; the ODPC website was under maintenance and could not be checked.
8. **Reform status (October 2026):** The NADCO-derived Elections (Amendment) (No. 2) Bill 2024 (Senate Bill No. 29 of 2024) would have replaced s.39; the National Assembly passed the Bill on 22 October 2026 with amendments that **deleted clause 22**, leaving s.39 as it stands; it has gone back to the Senate and is not law. ### 1.1 Article 81(e): principles of the electoral system [PRIMARY]

> 81. The electoral system shall comply with the following principles— ... (e) free and fair elections, which are— (i) by secret ballot; (ii) free from violence, intimidation, improper influence or corruption; (iii) conducted by an independent body; (iv) transparent; and (v) administered in an impartial, neutral, efficient, accurate and accountable manner.

Relevance: "transparent" and "accurate and accountable" are the constitutional anchors for publication of forms (transparency) and for identifying who completed them (accountability).

### 1.2 Article 86: voting [PRIMARY]

> 86. At every election, the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission shall ensure that— (a) whatever voting method is used, the system is simple, accurate, verifiable, secure, accountable and transparent; (b) the votes cast are counted, tabulated and the results announced promptly by the presiding officer at each polling station; (c) the results from the polling stations are openly and accurately collated and promptly announced by the returning officer; and (d) appropriate structures and mechanisms to eliminate electoral malpractice are put in place, including the safekeeping of election materials.

Relevance: (a) "verifiable" is the standard the Supreme Court applied in 2017 and 2022 to the forms and the transmission system; (b) fixes the polling station as the place of counting and announcement; (d) "safekeeping of election materials" grounds retention duties.

### 1.3 Article 88: the IEBC [PRIMARY]

> 88. (1) There is established the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission. ... (4) The Commission is responsible for conducting or supervising referenda and elections to any elective body or office established by this Constitution, and any other elections as prescribed by an Act of Parliament and, in particular, for— (a) the continuous registration of citizens as voters; (b) the regular revision of the voters' roll; (c) the delimitation of constituencies and wards; (d) the regulation of the process by which parties nominate candidates for elections; (e) the settlement of electoral disputes, including disputes relating to or arising from nominations but excluding election petitions and disputes subsequent to the declaration of election results; (f) the registration of candidates for election; (g) voter education; (h) the facilitation of the observation, monitoring and evaluation of elections; (i) the regulation of the amount of money that may be spent by or on behalf of a candidate or party in respect of any election; (j) the development of a code of conduct for candidates and parties contesting elections; and (k) the monitoring of compliance with the legislation required by Article 82 (1) (b) relating to nomination of candidates by parties. (5) The Commission shall exercise its powers and perform its functions in accordance with this Constitution and national legislation.

### 1.4 Article 138: procedure at presidential election [PRIMARY]

> 138. ... (3) In a presidential election— (a) all persons registered as voters for the purposes of parliamentary elections are entitled to vote; (b) the poll shall be taken by secret ballot on the day specified in Article 101(1) at the time, in the places and in the manner prescribed under an Act of Parliament; and (c) after counting the votes in the polling stations, the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission shall tally and verify the count and declare the result. (4) A candidate shall be declared elected as President if the candidate receives— (a) more than half of all the votes cast in the election; and (b) at least twenty-five per cent of the votes cast in each of more than half of the counties.

> 140. (1) A person may file a petition in the Supreme Court to challenge the election of the President-elect within seven days after the date of the declaration of the results of the presidential election. Reg. 83(3): decisions on validity of a ballot "shall be final except in an election petition".

**(corrected, see B-verification.md §4):** the paragraph above quotes the 2012 text; reg. 83 was substantially rewritten by LN 72/2017, r. 37 [PRIMARY]. As now in force, reg. 83(1) requires the constituency returning officer, "in the presence of candidates or agents and observers, if present", to "(a) tally the final results from each polling station in a constituency for the election of a member of the National Assembly and members of the county assembly; (b) disregard the results of the count of a polling station where the total valid votes exceeds the number of registered voters in that polling station; (c) disregard the results of the count of a polling station where the total votes exceeds the total number of voters who turned out to vote in that polling station; (d) collate and publicly announce to the persons present the results from each polling station in the constituency for the election of the President, county Governor, Senator and county women representative to the National Assembly; (e) complete the relevant Form 35B and 36B ...; (f) sign and date the relevant forms and publicly declare the results ...; (g) issue certificates ...; (h) deliver to the county returning officer the collated results ...; and (i) deliver to the Chairperson of the Commission the collated results for the election of the president to the national tallying centre." **Reg. 83(2) now reads: "The Chairperson of the Commission shall tally and verify the results received at the national tallying centre."** That is the provision *Okoiti* (2026) declared constitutional, read as a collective threshold-confirming function that cannot vary certified constituency figures. (The 2012 "subject to confirmation by the Commission" wording no longer exists.)

2012 reg. 87(2)(c): the returning officer shall "electronically transmit the provisional results to the Commission" — declared unconstitutional in *Maina Kiai* to the extent it made constituency results provisional. Reg. 87(4)(a) (2012): the Chairperson "hold[s] the certificate until the results of that election in every county have been received and thereafter publish[es] a notice in the Gazette within seven days declaring the person who has received the greatest number of votes ... to have been elected President".

In *Odinga & 16 others v Ruto & 10 others* [2022] KESC 56 the Supreme Court declared reg. 87(3) "unconstitutional to the extent that it purported to vest the power of verifying and tallying Presidential Election results, as received at the National Tallying Centre, solely on the Chairperson to the exclusion of other members of the Commission" [EXTRACT]. (Verification, now [PRIMARY]: reg. 87(1)(b) and 87(3)(a)–(g) confirmed verbatim in the Rev. 2017 consolidation ("[L.N. 72/2017, r. 39.]"), including "(d) sign and date the forms and make available a copy to any candidate or the national chief agent present" and "(g) deliver a written notification of the results to the Chief Justice and the incumbent president within seven days of the declaration". The reg. 87(3) holding is in the Supreme Court's judgment of **5 September 2022** (the document Kenya Law cites as [2022] KESC 54), para 50: "to the extent that Regulation 87 (3) of the Elections (General) Regulations purports to vest the power of verifying and tallying Presidential Election results, as received at the National Tallying Centre, solely on the Chairperson to the exclusion of other members of the Commission, the same is contrary to and inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution." ### 5.2 *Maina Kiai & 2 others v IEBC & 2 others* [2017] KEHC 8646 (7 April 2017) and *IEBC v Kiai & 5 others*, Civil Appeal 105 of 2017, [2017] KECA 477 (23 June 2017; Makhandia, Ouko, Kiage, M'inoti, Murgor JJA)

Court of Appeal, para. 140 [EXTRACT]: "It is clear beyond peradventure that the polling station is the true locus for the free exercise of the voters' will." Relevance: Form 34A is the primary document; everything upstream must be reconcilable to it.

### 5.3 *Odinga & another v IEBC & 2 others*, Presidential Election Petition 1 of 2017

Findings relevant to forms (all [EXTRACT] or [SECONDARY]; figures conflict between sources and should be checked in the judgment):

**(corrected, see B-verification.md §3.1) — the Supreme Court's own PDF of the 20 September 2017 judgment has now been read [PRIMARY] (coram Maraga CJ, Mwilu DCJ, Ojwang, Wanjala, Njoki and Lenaola SCJJ; "[405] By a majority of four (with J. B. Ojwang and N. S. Ndungu, SCJJ dissenting)"). The authoritative passages are:**
- Para 343: the IEBC produced "1 Form 34C, 291 Forms 34B, and 41,451 Forms 34As"; the Registrar observed that some forms "appeared to be carbon copies", some "appeared to be photocopies" and "Some of the forms had no evidence of being stamped or signed."

(Para 368 restates the 269 as "not stamped"; the judgment itself is inconsistent on that figure.)
- Paras 336, 354, 362 (stamps and security features not legally required): "The respondents however contended that hand over notes and official stamps were not a legal requirement"; "the only requirement under Regulations 79 (2) (a) and 83 (a) is for the signing of the forms and that there was no requirement for security features"; "though there is no specific provision requiring the forms to have watermarks and serial numbers as security features, there is no plausible explanation for this discrepancy".
- Paras 376–377 (weight of signatures and features): "of the 290 Forms 34B that were used to declare the final results, 56 of them had no security features? ... - Para 300 (the holding on Forms 34A/34B): the chairperson "declared the final results for the election of the president, before the 1st respondent had received all the results from Forms 34A from all the 40,883 polling stations contrary to the Constitution and the applicable electoral law ... declared, the said results solely, on the basis of Forms 34B, some of which were of dubious authenticity ... the 1st respondent in disregard of the provisions of Section 39 (1C), of the Elections Act, either failed, or neglected to electronically transmit, in the prescribed form, the tabulated results". Para 298: the complementary mechanism "did not appear to us to meet the yardsticks of verifiability inbuilt in the Constitution and Section 44A". (The phrases "were not verifiable as demanded by the constitution" and "could not possibly have verified all 40,883" in the bullets below do not appear in the judgment.)
- The dissent of Ndung'u SCJ is not in this document and could not be found elsewhere; the bullet on it below stays [SECONDARY].

- Kenya Law summary: the 1st respondent "committed irregularities in transmitting results" and those irregularities "impugned the integrity of the entire presidential election"; no misconduct by the 3rd respondent; the declared result "invalid, null and void".
- The petitioners' case was that Forms 34A and 34B "lacked the names of Returning Officers; some lacked the IEBC authentication stamp; some were not signed by the candidates' agents", that results on the portal differed from the forms, and that the chairperson declared on Forms 34B "before receiving all requisite Forms 34A". The IEBC answered that "neither the Elections Act nor the Election (General) Regulations requires that Forms 34A should bear the 1st respondent's stamp", that agents' failure to sign did not invalidate results, that "all Forms 34A and 34B were signed and/or stamped and that their integrity was not compromised", and that the forms carried security features such as guilloche patterns.
- Registrar's scrutiny (Esther Nyaiyaki): a random sample of 4,299 Forms 34A from five counties was checked for watermarks, serial numbers, signatures, stamps and agents' signatures. Secondary reports of the majority's recital: 481 were carbon copies, 269 lacked stamps, 11 lacked watermarks, 46 photocopies were unsigned (The Kenya Times); another report: "269 original copies that were not signed and 31 forms that were not signed by the returning officers". For the 290/291 Forms 34B: 56 had no security features, 31 lacked serial numbers, 5 were unsigned, 2 were stamped but not signed (Oxford Human Rights Hub blog, https://ohrh.law.ox.ac.uk/the-kenyan-supreme-court-overturns-presidential-election-results-of-forms-34as-bs-and-cs/). The IEBC did not provide usable access to the server holding Forms 34A or to server logs; 11,883 Forms 34A were reported unavailable for scrutiny nine days after the declaration.
- The majority held that declaring on Forms 34B without the Forms 34A and without the chairperson verifying against them meant the results "were not verifiable as demanded by the constitution"; it agreed with *Maina Kiai* on Form 34A's primacy and found the national returning officer "could not possibly have verified all 40,883" Forms 34A in the time taken.
- Ndung'u SCJ (dissent) examined the forms herself and found the disputed Forms 34A and 34B "proper in form and content", with signatures and security features "contrary to the findings of the report".
- Commentators note the majority did not nullify "because one presiding officer forgot a stamp"; the absence of stamps, signatures and security features mattered as cumulative evidence that the forms could not be authenticated and the transmission chain could not be verified.

Relevance: this is the leading authority for the proposition that **signatures, stamps, watermarks and serial numbers on Forms 34A/34B are indicia of authenticity whose absence can, in aggregate, defeat verifiability**.

### 5.8 *Kenya Human Rights Commission & another v Chebukati & 2 others*, Petition E400 of 2022, [2025] KEHC 8 (KLR), 9 January 2025 (Mugambi J, per news; Kenya Law header does not show the judge in extracts)

Public-officer status: presiding officers and returning officers are election officials of a constitutional commission performing public functions under Articles 86 and 88; their names and signatures on the forms are the mechanism of accountability the Constitution contemplates ("accountable", Art. 81(e)(v), 86(a)). ## 8. Analysis: how far redaction is compatible with the duties to publish transparently and verifiably

This section applies the law above to the tool described in `README.md`, `DESIGN.md` and `VERIFYING.md` (signatures banded through the middle; 3 characters of each ID/passport hidden, or all with `--full-ids`; 3 of the last 6 digits of phone numbers hidden, or all with `--full-phones`; names, party names, results, QR codes, station codes and stamps visible; `--mask-stamps` optional; cryptographic proof that only listed 8x8 blocks changed and results/stamps are pixel-identical to the original).

### 8.1 Whose duty is publication, and what must be published

- The statutory publication duty (s.39(1C)(c)) binds **the Commission**; it is satisfied by the IEBC's own portal during the results period (*KHRC v Chebukati*). A third party republishing forms is not discharging s.39 and is not bound by it; equally, nothing in the Act or Regulations regulates the content of such republication. The relevant constraints on a third party are general law: defamation/forgery and election-offence provisions on falsifying or publishing false results (not researched here), the Data Protection Act, 2019 (research track A), and the practical need not to mislead readers about what a form shows.
- The statute says "publish the polling result forms", not "publish unredacted images". The 2012 prescribed Form 34 (the only Schedule I could read) contains **no ID or telephone fields**, so those fields are not part of what the law historically defined as the declaration. The forms' legal content is the results, the station identifiers, the declaration text, and the **names and signatures** of the officials and agents.
  **(corrected, see B-verification.md §1.1):** since 21 April 2017 the prescribed Form 34A (LN 72/2017, r. 44) **does** include "ID/Passport No." and "Tel. Contact" columns for agents and candidates, and Forms 34B/34C add officers' ID numbers. The historical point about the 2012 form remains true, but the current legal content of a Form 34A includes those identifiers. That strengthens the argument that an unredacted image is the "polling result form" the IEBC must publish under s.39(1C)(c); it does not change the position that the statute is silent on redaction and says nothing about third-party republication, and it must still be read with IEBC Act s.27(6), the Data Protection Act and Articles 24 and 31(c). The IEBC itself publishes the forms unredacted (verified on the live portal, §4.3).

### 8.2 What "verifiable" and "transparent" mean in the case law

- Verifiability has consistently meant **traceability of a declared result to the polling-station record and the ability to compare the record at each level** (Art. 86; *Maina Kiai*; 2017 and 2022 petitions; *Omtatah* 2026; *Katiba* on live-streaming as a comparison tool). None of the courts' verification exercises depended on reading an agent's ID number or phone number, or on handwriting comparison of signatures.
- Signatures, stamps and security features were used as **presence/absence indicators of authenticity** (2017 Registrar's checklist: watermarks, serial numbers, signatures, stamps, agents' signatures; 2022: UV ink, micro-text, tapered serialisation, anti-copy, watermark, digital signatures, QR code). A form that is signed and stamped is treated as authenticated; one that is unsigned by the presiding officer, unstamped and lacking security features is suspect. The dissent in 2017 and the lower courts show the test is cumulative and material, not mechanical.
- Transparency in 2022 operated through **image-to-original comparison** by the Registrar and through agents' own carbon copies.

### 8.3 Fit of the tool's choices with that framework

1. **Signatures banded through the middle, with the top and bottom of strokes visible.** The legally significant fact — that the presiding officer (whose signature is mandatory, reg. 79(1)) and the deputy and agents signed, or did not — remains visible. No court has used signature-stroke comparison on published forms; verification by courts is done on originals or certified copies, which the tool does not touch. The tool's claim that "the form was signed" remains checkable. Residual risk: a reader cannot detect a **forged** signature from the redacted image; but neither could the public from the unredacted image without exemplars, and courts verify against originals. The Act's definition of "biometric" includes "signatures" (s.2), which supports treating signatures as personal data warranting protection rather than as public content. Recommendation: keep the default band; do not offer a "full black-out" of signatures that would hide whether a line is signed at all, because an unsigned presiding-officer line is legally meaningful (reg. 79(1); 2017 petition).
2. **Names left visible.** This matches the accountability function the Regulations assign to names (reg. 79, Form 34 Schedule: "Name: … Signature: …"; reg. 74 name lodged with the presiding officer; reg. 83(1)(d) returning officer signs and dates). Removing names would undercut Art. 81(e)(v)/86 accountability; leaving them is consistent with every source found.
3. **ID/passport numbers and phone numbers partially or fully hidden.** No regulation I could verify requires these on a declaration form or requires their publication; reg. 74 requires only name and address to be lodged privately with the presiding officer. **(corrected, see B-verification.md §1.1):** the first clause is now wrong — the Schedule substituted by LN 72/2017 prescribes "ID/Passport No." and "Tel. Contact" columns on Forms 34A, 34B and 34C, so these entries are part of the prescribed declaration. What remains true is that no provision requires their *publication* as such (s.39(1C)(c) says only "publish the polling result forms"), that reg. 74 lodges name and address privately, and that no court has used them for verification. The redaction question therefore turns on data-protection law (track A) and Articles 24 and 31(c), not on whether the fields are "part of the form": they are. The IEBC has itself treated ID and phone numbers as sensitive personal identifiers when releasing electoral records (Kibra 2019), s.27(6) of the IEBC Act binds it to data-protection principles, and Art. 31(c) protects against information being "unnecessarily ... revealed". Hiding them does not impair any verification function described in the case law. The partial-masking default (3 of 8 digits) leaves a small candidate set and may still identify; that is a data-protection question for track A, but from the electoral-law side nothing favours showing them at all. `--full-ids`/`--full-phones` is the safer default from this track's standpoint.
4. **Stamps never masked (default).** The 2022 Gazette procedure requires the presiding officer to "sign and stamp" the original and carbon copies; stamps were on the 2017 and 2022 Registrar's checklists and are read by courts and observers as authenticity marks; KHRC's 2022 complaint singled out forms "neither signed nor stamped". (Corrected, see B-verification.md §6.4 and §8: the KHRC/Kura Yangu Sauti Yangu complaint dates from 10 August 2017; the stamp is required by Gazette Notices 9280 and 9540 of 2022 (KIEMS step 14(c): "the Form 34A has been stamped"), not by the Act or Regulations, and the prescribed Form 34A has no stamp field; the Supreme Court in 2017 recorded that "official stamps were not a legal requirement" while still treating unstamped and unsigned forms as evidence of doubtful authenticity (paras 336, 350, 376–377).) Keeping stamps pixel-identical is well aligned. **`--mask-stamps` runs against this**: it removes an authenticity indicator the courts and civil society rely on and should be used only where a stamp genuinely overprints an identifier and the trade-off is documented in the manifest (which the tool does).
5. **QR codes, serial numbers, station codes and results untouched, with cryptographic proof against the original.** This maps onto the 2022 Court's method (portal image v original; security features including tapered serialisation and digital signatures) and onto Art. 86(a) "verifiable". The verification guide's reliance on the IEBC portal copy as "the original" needs a caveat after *KHRC v Chebukati*: the portal closes about 90 days after the election, so the "original" for later verification will be a copy obtained under reg. 93, an Access to Information request, or a court order, or an agent's carbon copy; the manifest's SHA-256 of the KIEMS transmission file remains the anchor. (Corrected, see B-verification.md §6.3: in October 2026 the 2022 forms are available again on `forms.iebc.or.ke`, so the portal copy can currently be fetched and hashed; the caveat still applies because the IEBC is under no duty to keep it there.)
6. **Agents' "reasons for refusal to sign" and officers' "statutory comments".** These free-text fields are part of the prescribed form and are legally meaningful (reg. 79(3)–(4)). The tool reads them by OCR only to catch stray numbers and signatures; it should not mask the text itself. Where an agent writes a phone number or ID in that field, masking the digits is consistent with the analysis above.
7. **What the law does not say.** I found no statute, regulation, Gazette notice, IEBC policy, ODPC guidance text, or judgment that addresses redaction of published result forms, for or against. The IEBC has never redacted; nobody has complained about identifiers on the portal; the complaints have been about completeness, legibility and missing signatures/stamps. (Verification: "the IEBC has never redacted" is now confirmed by inspection of a 2022 Form 34A on the live portal [PRIMARY]; the "missing signatures/stamps" complaint is from 2017 (corrected, see B-verification.md §6.4); the ODPC's guidance could not be read because its site was down; *Okoiti* (2026) declined the Article 35 prayers about "the manner of publication of results" without addressing personal data.) A redacted republication that is complete, legible, shows signatures-as-signed and stamps, and proves identity of results with the original therefore answers every published criticism of the IEBC's own portal while removing data no verification function uses.

### 8.4 Residual legal risks to flag to the decision-maker

- **Mis-description risk.** Publishing a modified image of a statutory form invites the 2022-style allegation that forms were "doctored". The tool's manifest, ledger and verifier are the answer; the published page should state plainly that the image is a redacted copy and where the unredacted original can be obtained (IEBC portal while open; Access to Information request; agents' copies).
- **Election offences.** I did not research the Election Offences Act, 2016 for offences relating to publishing or altering election documents; this should be checked before a public release (s.6 concerns Commission staff; other sections may concern "any person").
- **Period of publication.** Nothing requires or prohibits a permanent third-party archive. *KHRC v Chebukati* confirms the IEBC need not keep one; it does not say a third party may not.
- **Unverified Schedule text.** If the 2017 Schedule expressly prescribes ID and phone columns for agents, that strengthens the argument that they are part of the "form" the IEBC must publish, but it still says nothing about third-party republication and must be read with IEBC Act s.27(6) and Art. 31(c). **(corrected, see B-verification.md §1.1): the 2017 Schedule does prescribe them (LN 72/2017, r. 44).** The risk flagged here is therefore live: a reader may argue that a form with those columns hidden is not "the polling result form". The answer remains that s.39(1C)(c) binds the IEBC, not third parties; that the IEBC's own duty under s.27(6) of its Act is to manage personal data under the Data Protection Act's principles; and that the tool's manifest and verifier make plain exactly which pixels were changed.
- **Election Offences Act (now examined, see B-verification.md §11).** No provision expressly addresses publishing an altered copy of a result form. The provisions to consider are s.13(d) (any person who "interferes with election material by removing destroying, concealing or mutilating ... any such material save on the authority of the Commission or under the provisions of the Elections Act"), s.13(e) (printing or supplying "any election material in connection with the election save on the authority of the Commission") and s.17 (computer-system offences). A redacted copy published as such, with the original untouched and identified, is not obviously within any of them, but this should be confirmed by counsel before release.
