## What changed after verification

Seven corrections from the 31 October 2026 verification pass bear on this report. Each is applied in the areas named; the rest of the report stands.

1. ***ODM v IEBC* (Kibra) [2019] KEHC 2492 is authority against severance, not for it (CORRECTED, A-verification C1).** The High Court held that the IEBC "has not demonstrated how Article 31 of the Constitution of Kenya is" infringed, found that "for free and fair election to be had, national I/D or passport numbers provide" the needed particulars, and issued mandamus "compelling the Respondent to publish the Kibra Constituency voters' register containing the names, National ID and Passport numbers". The IEBC had released a register with "identification numbers (truncated)" and lost. Every use of "Kibra" as support for masking identifiers (section 1, areas 2.3, 2.5, 2.15, the decision table, open point 4) has been rewritten. The case for masking now rests on DPA ss.25(d), 41(3) and 51(1), ATI s.6(1)(d) and Art. 24(1)(e). It follows, and is now said plainly, that an Art. 35 request by a party or candidate for the unredacted forms might succeed. Press reports still conflict (B-verification §12 records the Nation and Standard on the "minus personal details" side); the judgment's own order, read in Kenya Law snippets, governs.
2. **The agents' "ID/Passport No." and "Tel. Contact" columns are prescribed (CORRECTED, B-verification §1.1).** The Schedule substituted by LN 72/2017 r.44 prescribes them on Forms 34A, 34B and 34C; Forms 34B and 34C also carry the returning officer's and chairperson's "ID Number". The identifiers are part of the statutory form, not an IEBC design choice. The necessity argument in areas 2.3 and 2.8 is reworded: the question is no longer whether the fields belong on the form but whether a republisher must show them, and the honest answer is that the IEBC's own publication of the full form is lawful on its face, while a third party's purpose does not need the identifiers.
3. **`forms.iebc.or.ke` is online on 31 October 2026 and serves the 2022 forms unredacted (CORRECTED, B-verification §6.3).** A 2022 presidential Form 34A downloaded from it shows agents' names, eight-digit ID numbers, ten-digit phone numbers, signatures and the stamps, nothing blurred. The IEBC has never redacted. The portal was closed about 90 days after the 2022 election (*KHRC v Chebukati*, SECONDARY) but has since re-opened; it is under no duty to stay open. Areas 2.10, 2.11, 2.13 and 2.15 now say "if the portal closes again" rather than "after the portal closes", and area 2.11 adds the consequence that, while the IEBC publishes the same identifiers, the "real risk of harm" and reg. 37(3)(a) "publicly available" questions cut against treating every leak on the redacted copy as notifiable.
4. **Stamps are not a legal requirement (CORRECTED, B-verification §1.1, §3.1, §8).** The prescribed Forms 34A and 34B have no stamp field; the stamp comes only from Gazette Notices 9280 and 9540 of 2022; the 2017 Supreme Court recorded that "official stamps were not a legal requirement" (para 336) while still counting unstamped and unsigned forms as evidence of doubtful authenticity (paras 350, 376–377) [PRIMARY]. The sentence previously quoted as the IEBC deponent's does not exist in the judgment and has been removed. Election Petition Appeal 3 of 2018 is STILL UNVERIFIED (second pass: it is *Ojuok v Okal & 2 others* [2018] KEHC 3736, para 24, CONFIRMED [PRIMARY]). The Kura Yangu Sauti Yangu "neither signed nor stamped" complaint is from 10 August 2017, not 2022. Area 2.6 is re-weighed: the stamp's evidentiary role stands at PRIMARY level, its legal weight is lower than first stated, and T2 (mask the band under the stamp, keep the ring verifiable) is now the clearer choice; the case for T1 (never mask under a stamp) is weaker.
5. **Legislation (A-verification A1, A3, G1; B-verification §2.4).** The Data Protection (Amendment) Bill 2025 is not before Parliament (it is a civil-society draft; the "in force 5 Oct 2026" claim has no primary support) and is removed from the watch list. Reg. 26 is CONFIRMED on Kenya Law (heading "Requirement for specified processing to be done in Kenya"; reg. 2 defines neither "strategic interest" nor "public good"). The Elections (Amendment) (No. 2) Bill's server-location clause was deleted by the National Assembly on 22 October 2026 (Hansard, CONFIRMED), so reg. 26 is the only localisation rule. The National Assembly on 15 October 2026 adopted a committee report calling on the "IEBC to expedite its registration and full compliance" with the ODPC (Hansard, CONFIRMED): area 2.9 treats the IEBC's registration as incomplete.
6. **Confirmed, which strengthens several areas.** Elections (Technology) Regulations reg. 2 "biometric … and signatures" (verbatim from the Gazette copy) and the identical Elections Act s.2 definition [PRIMARY] (area 2.2); reg. 37 (A's reconstruction was right), reg. 49 and s.31(5) (areas 2.3, 2.8, 2.11); the *Katiba* 2021 and *Worldcoin* 2025 orders (area 2.8); the penalty notices, with Regus reduced to KES 2.5m in [2025] KEHC 13491 (area 2.11); the existence and draft status of the s.47 notice (consultation to 25 September 2026), text unread (area 2.4); the 2022 Supreme Court's portal-against-originals finding, para 20(d) [PRIMARY] (area 2.13); the Election Offences Act, now examined: s.13(d)–(e) and s.17 are the provisions to consider (areas 2.13, open point 11).
7. **Still unverified, and a new lead.** All ODPC documents (guidance notes, register, determinations, the s.47 draft text); several court passages (the 26 September 2022 reasoned judgment, the Mue orders, the lower-court stamp cases). New: *Gachoka & 3 others v Cabinet Secretary, National Treasury & 11 others* [2026] KEHC 13493 (15 September 2026) discusses reg. 26 and the petitioners' argument that Safaricom hosts IEBC systems including the results transmission network; the court's disposition was not reachable (area 2.1, open point 7). (The court passages and *Gachoka* were read on the second pass, below.)

9. **Reg. 37(1)(a) does not reach a form leak by itself.** Full text: a breach is deemed to carry real risk of harm where it relates to "the data subject's full name or identification number **and** any of the personal data or classes of personal data relating to the data subject set out in the Second Schedule". So an ID, phone or signature leaking on a redacted page is not a deemed notifiable breach; whether s.43(1)'s "real risk of harm" exists is the controller's assessment, recorded each time. The 72-hour clock, once that risk exists, is unchanged (area 2.11; open point 3).
10. **Reg. 41(2) is narrower and says "on request".** "Where a transfer of data takes place in reliance on subregulation (1)— (a) the transfer shall be documented; (b) the documentation shall be provided to the Commissioner on request; and (c) the documentation shall include— (i)–(iv)". The duty attaches to safeguards-based transfers; the record goes to the ODPC when asked. A1's `transfers.jsonl` record stays right (s.48(a)–(b) and reg. 5(3) call for proof anyway); it should be exportable on request rather than filed per transfer. The 2026 Guidance's notification rule is still unread (area 2.1).
11. **The ATI (General) Regulations 2023 provide for redaction.** LN 161 of 2023: reg. 8(1)(d) (the Information Access Officer reviews requested information against s.6, "redacting or withholding information that may be limited in accordance with that section"), reg. 22(2)(h), reg. 25(8)(b) ("granting partial or redacted access"). New: ATI s.28(10) makes it an offence to convey information obtained under the Act while concealing "part of the information … with intent to deceive" — a reason for the redacted set to keep saying plainly that it is redacted (section 1; area 2.15; open point 13).
12. ***Odhiambo* (2026) is a holding on phone numbers.** Para 95(2): "registered mobile phone number constitutes a digital identifier linking personal data that relates to an individual's private affairs hence qualifies for protection under Article 31 (c) & (d)". The "digital identity just like the physical national ID" words were the petitioners' prayer. LN 163 of 2015 is revoked; the SIM rules are LN 90 of 2025 (regs. 8(d), 19(1)) (area 2.3).

## 1. Where the two frameworks meet

**The duty to publish is real, statutory and narrow.** Elections Act s.39(1C)(c) requires the Commission, for a presidential election, to "publish the polling result forms on an online public portal maintained by the Commission" (A: VERIFIED; B: [PRIMARY], Rev. 2019 edition and the gazetted 2016 Act read in full; unchallenged in *Katiba Institute* 2018/2023 and confirmed in force by the IEBC's 2026 press release and by *Okoiti v IEBC* [2026] KEHC 13916, which declined to quash s.39(1C)). Its constitutional roots are Art. 81(e)(iv)–(v) ("transparent", "accurate and accountable") and Art. 86(a) ("verifiable, secure, accountable and transparent") [PRIMARY, Parliament's Constitution PDF]. Reg. 79(2A)(d) separately requires a copy of the declaration to be affixed at the polling-station entrance [PRIMARY]. Nothing in the Act, the Regulations or the Technology Regulations defines what a published "form" must show, requires unredacted publication, or regulates republication by third parties (B §0.3(1), §3.2; the Technology Regulations have no Part on transmission or the portal at all, CORRECTED). **But the form itself is prescribed, and it prescribes the identifiers**: the Schedule substituted by LN 72/2017 r.44 gives Form 34A an "Agents or Candidates (if present)" table with the columns "No. | Name of Candidate or Agent | ID/Passport No. | Party Name/Independent Candidate | Tel. Contact | Signature | Date", and Forms 34B and 34C add officers' "ID Number" [PRIMARY, CORRECTED]. The 2012 Form 34 had no such fields, but that is history. And the IEBC publishes the prescribed form whole: on 31 October 2026 `forms.iebc.or.ke` serves the 2022 Forms 34A with agents' ID numbers, phone numbers and signatures legible [PRIMARY, CORRECTED]. So the statutory duty, as the Commission discharges it, is to publish an image that carries those identifiers; the statute is simply silent on whether it, or anyone else, may publish less.

**The duty to protect is equally real and is not switched off by the duty to publish.** Art. 31(c) protects against private information being "*unnecessarily* required or revealed" (VERIFIED), and Art. 24(1)(e) asks whether "less restrictive means" exist (VERIFIED). The Data Protection Act's exemption for processing where "disclosure is required by or under any written law" (s.51(2)(c)) is bounded by s.51(1): "Nothing in this Part shall exempt any data controller or data processor from complying with data protection principles relating to lawful processing, minimisation of collection, data quality, and adopting security safeguards" (VERIFIED). So s.25(d) (data "limited to what is necessary") and s.41(3) (by default "only personal data which is necessary for each specific purpose is processed") keep binding even when the publication itself is compelled. Regs. 54–57 confirm there is no electoral-body exemption (VERIFIED).

**The IEBC Act puts both duties in one section.** s.27(1): "The Commission shall publish and publicise all important information within its mandate affecting the nation." s.27(6): "The Commission shall ensure the management of personal data is in accordance with the principles of personal data protection as set out in the Data Protection Act" [PRIMARY-mirror]. The legislature placed the publication duty and the personal-data duty side by side and subordinated neither. Read with s.51(1), the only coherent position is: publish the form, minimise the personal data on it.

**Severance is available under the Access to Information Act, but the one electoral case went the other way.** The ATI Act has no express severance clause (A §8.1; STILL UNVERIFIED against the official text), but it ties the privacy exemption to specific "information" whose disclosure would "involve the unwarranted invasion of the privacy of an individual" (s.6(1)(d), VERIFIED), interprets everything "on the basis of a duty to disclose" (s.4(4)) and weights the public-interest override toward accountability of public entities and informed debate (s.6(4), (6)). Kenya Law anonymises personal data in the judgments it is bound to publish (*Tumaz v National Council for Law Reporting* [2022] KEHC 14747, CORRECTED name; holding not re-read). (Corrected, second pass: *Tumaz* dismissed a privacy claim against Kenya Law for publishing a ruling naming the applicants, anonymisation being confined to children, sexual offences, family matters and granted applications.) The **Access to Information (General) Regulations 2023 (LN 161 of 2023)**, however, do provide for redaction: the Information Access Officer reviews requested information against s.6, "redacting or withholding information that may be limited in accordance with that section" (reg. 8(1)(d)), and the Commission may order "partial or redacted access" (reg. 25(8)(b)) (corrected, second pass, A-verification C20). **The electoral precedent, however, is adverse.** In *ODM v IEBC* (Kibra, [2019] KEHC 2492) the IEBC had released the voters' register with "identification numbers (truncated)" to protect confidentiality; the High Court held that the IEBC "has not demonstrated how Article 31 of the Constitution of Kenya is" infringed, found that "for free and fair election to be had, national I/D or passport numbers provide" the needed particulars, and compelled publication of the register "containing the names, National ID and Passport numbers" (CORRECTED, A-verification C1; the earlier "minus personal details" reading came from press reports, which still conflict). The same order **withheld telephone numbers** as "confidential information … which may be injurious to voters" protected by Art. 31 (order (b); corrected, second pass): the one electoral precedent keeps ID numbers and severs phone numbers. The Supreme Court in *Mue v IEBC* [2017] KESC 45 said that "information held by the State or State organs, unless for very exceptional circumstances, ought to be freely shared with the public", but expunged IEBC memos obtained outside the statutory route (s.27 IEBC Act, ATI Act) as a violation of the IEBC's privacy (paras 22–27; orders CONFIRMED, second pass). Two consequences. First, the legal footing for masking is **not** a severance precedent; it is the Data Protection Act's own minimisation and security duties (ss.25(d), 41(3), 51(1)), the ATI privacy exemption (s.6(1)(d)) and the Art. 24(1)(e) "less restrictive means" test, applied to the *republisher's* purpose. Second, **an Art. 35 request by a party, candidate or citizen for the unredacted forms might well succeed** as to ID numbers, as it did for the register in Kibra (phone numbers were withheld there); the redacted set does not and cannot bar that route, and should say so (areas 2.12, 2.15).

**The courts' authenticity tests do not need the data the tool removes.** Verifiability means tracing a declared result back to the polling-station record (Art. 86; *Maina Kiai*, as adopted by the Supreme Court in 2017 at para 264 [PRIMARY]; the 2017 and 2022 presidential petitions; *Okoiti v IEBC* [2026] KEHC 13916, "Documentary Primacy (constituency Forms 34A/34B remain the sole point of truth)" [Kenya Law case note]). The 2017 Registrar checked "the presence of a watermark using the UV reader; colour of the forms; serialization; Microtext; X10 magnification; Column for comments on the form; Format of the forms; and Anti-copy" (para 344) and reported forms with "no evidence of being stamped or signed" (para 343) [PRIMARY]; the majority asked whether forms without security features could "be still considered genuine" and called the officer's signature "the last solemn act of assurance to the voter" (paras 376–377) [PRIMARY]. The 2022 Court found the originals "exactly the same as those on the Public Portal" (para 20(d)) [PRIMARY]; the list of 2022 security features comes from the 26 September reasoned judgment, which could not be obtained [EXTRACT] (second pass: now read — "UV sensitive security features; Microtext …, tapered serialization, anti-copy features and water mark", para 148 [PRIMARY]). No court read a signature's strokes, an agent's ID or a phone number to authenticate a form (B §8.2). The legally meaningful facts are: *is* the presiding officer's line signed (mandatory, reg. 79(1) [PRIMARY]); are the agents' lines signed or marked refused (reg. 79(3)–(7) [PRIMARY]); is the stamp there (required only by Gazette Notices 9280 and 9540 of 2022 [PRIMARY]; the prescribed form has no stamp field, and the 2017 Court recorded that "official stamps were not a legal requirement", para 336 [PRIMARY, CORRECTED]); are the results and serial/QR identical to the original.

**Where that leaves the tool.** A redacted copy that keeps names, party, results, serial, QR and stamps pixel-identical, shows every signature line as signed or not, hides the stroke pattern and the identifiers, and proves block by block that nothing else changed is the Art. 24(1)(e) "less restrictive means" made concrete. Two honest qualifications after verification. The IEBC's own, lawful publication is of the full prescribed form; the redacted copy is a *republisher's* minimisation choice under the DPA, not what the electoral law requires of the Commission, and a reader may argue that a form with prescribed columns hidden is not "the polling result form" (B §8.4). And the identifiers the tool hides are, while the IEBC portal is up, one click away in the Commission's own copy; the redaction's value is that it does not *add* a second, permanent, third-party source of them. The residual conflicts are therefore not between publish and protect; they are (i) *where* the processing happens (reg. 26 localisation and ss.48–49 transfers), (ii) whether partial masking is enough minimisation given the re-identification arithmetic, (iii) whether the stamp's authenticity role should ever yield to the identifier under it, and (iv) the organisational duties (DPIA, records, contracts, breach handling) that no amount of good masking substitutes for. Sections 2.1–2.18 take these one at a time.

## 2. Areas

### 2.1 Data localisation and cross-border transfer

**Requirement and risk.**
- Any upload of a page bearing personal data to a server outside Kenya is a "transfer": s.25(h) ("not transferred outside Kenya, unless there is proof of adequate data protection safeguards or consent") and s.48 (VERIFIED). A foreign model host processing Kenyans' data is within the Act as a processor (s.4(b)(ii), VERIFIED). The transfer must be documented under reg. 41(2): "(i) the date and time of the transfer; (ii) the name of the recipient; (iii) the justification for the transfer; and (iv) a description of the personal data transferred" (VERIFIED). (Corrected, second pass: reg. 41(2) applies "Where a transfer of data takes place in reliance on subregulation (1)" — appropriate safeguards — and "(b) the documentation shall be provided to the Commissioner on request"; for transfers on necessity or consent, s.48's "proof to the Data Commissioner" and reg. 5(3) still call for the same record.) Reg. 42's deemed safeguards (Malabo Convention ratification, reciprocal agreement, BCRs) do not cover a US host (A §9.3(4)). The 2026 ODPC Cross-border Guidance Notes reportedly treat cloud storage abroad as a transfer and require **notification to the ODPC** (STILL UNVERIFIED as to text: the ODPC site was down; secondary accounts conflict on whether notification is per transfer or "on request", A-verification A2).
- **Reg. 26** (CONFIRMED verbatim on Kenya Law, A-verification A1; heading "Requirement for specified processing to be done in Kenya") prescribes, under s.50, that a controller or processor processing personal data "for the purpose of … (b) facilitating the conduct of elections for the representation of the people under the Constitution" shall "(a) process such personal data through a server and data centre located in Kenya; or (b) store at least one serving copy of the concerned personal data in a data centre located in Kenya." The list is introduced by "includes" (not closed); reg. 2 defines neither "strategic interest of the state" nor "public good" (CONFIRMED by index search, A-verification A1). The rule is satisfied by *either* limb on its face, but the ODPC's 2026 guidance is reported to read it strictly for election data (commentary, STILL UNVERIFIED). Whether a third-party republisher's processing is "for the purpose of facilitating the conduct of elections" is unresolved; A §9.1 advises a cautious operator to assume it is. Parliament has declined to add a statutory server-location rule: the Elections (Amendment) (No. 2) Bill's proposed s.44(4A)(iv) ("processing of data is only effected through a server or data centre located in Kenya") was deleted by the National Assembly on 22 October 2026 (Hansard, CONFIRMED), so reg. 26 is the only localisation rule for election data. A new lead: *Gachoka & 3 others v Cabinet Secretary, National Treasury & 11 others* [2026] KEHC 13493 (15 September 2026) describes reg. 26 as imposing localisation "expressly including 'facilitating the conduct of elections'" and records the petitioners' argument that Safaricom hosts IEBC systems including the results transmission network; the court's disposition of that argument was not reachable (STILL UNVERIFIED). (Corrected, second pass: that description is the respondents' submission (para 249); the court did not construe reg. 26; it quashed the divestiture under Art. 238, noting it "was not controverted that Safaricom hosts sensitive systems such as the IEBC's election transmission network" (para 709). No judicial reading of reg. 26 exists yet.)
- **Sensitive data abroad:** s.49(1): "The processing of sensitive personal data out of Kenya shall only be effected upon obtaining consent of a data subject and on obtaining confirmation of appropriate safeguards" (VERIFIED). If signature images are biometric (area 2.2), every held page sent abroad needs each signatory's consent, which is not obtainable at scale. s.45 offers no public-task ground for sensitive data (VERIFIED).
- **Contract:** s.42(2)(b) requires a written controller–processor contract; reg. 24(2) lists its contents (instructions, confidentiality, security, deletion or return on termination, audit); reg. 25(1): no sub-processor "without the prior authorisation of the data controller" (all VERIFIED). Ollama Cloud's standard terms are unlikely to meet reg. 24(2) without negotiation.
- **A second localisation rule** may apply: LN 44 of 2024 reg. 28 (critical information infrastructure must be domiciled in Kenya, offshore location by committee approval in Form CMCA 3) (VERIFIED text) if the IEBC results system is gazetted CII under CMCA s.9 (UNVERIFIED).
- Case law: *Republic v Tools for Humanity Corporation & 9 others; Katiba Institute & 4 others (ex parte)* [2025] KEHC 5629 (Worldcoin) treated s.48 as prohibiting transfer without the required safeguards, found no DPIA "contrary to section 31", and by mandamus ordered the data permanently erased within 7 days under the Data Commissioner's supervision (CONFIRMED, A-verification D3).

**Today.** Four distinct paths send pixels to a model:

| Path | What is sent | Where | Guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| `--vlm-audit` (finder + checker) | the redacted page, then zoomed crops around spots | Ollama Cloud by default (`OLLAMA_API_KEY` set → `https://ollama.com`), or `--vlm-host` | none needed by design: "only redacted pages are sent" |
| `--orient` | the redacted page at ≤1024 px, up to four turns | Clef on Ollama Cloud | none |
| `--vlm-fallback`, `propose` | the **unredacted** page | any host | refused for non-local hosts unless `--allow-remote-unredacted` |
| Harness `held_pages: cloud` (design) | held pages: a 512 px triage view with found fields hidden, then **full resolution** to locate fields | Ollama Cloud | approved once per job in the job file; each image hashed in the journal |

"Local" in `vlm.rs` is `LOCAL_HOSTS = ["localhost", "127.0.0.1", "::1"]`. A GPU box on the operator's own LAN in Nairobi (`http://10.0.0.5:11434`) is treated as remote: the guard fires and the default model switches to the cloud default. The manifest records for each review the model, the **hostname only** (`host: "ollama.com"`), prompt hashes and the SHA-256 of each image sent (`vlm_review`, `orientation`). It does not record a transfer justification, the recipient's legal identity or the country, and nothing records whether a serving copy stayed in Kenya.

**Gap.** (a) Redacted pages still carry names, party, stamps, serial numbers and partly masked identifiers: personal data. Every `--vlm-audit` and `--orient` call to `ollama.com` is a transfer needing a s.48 basis and reg. 41(2) documentation; the manifest records three of the four reg. 41(2) elements (time via `created_at`, recipient hostname, description via the image hash and area) but not the justification, and not in a form a DPO can hand to the ODPC. (b) Held pages at full resolution show unredacted IDs, phones and signatures; under the conservative reading of signatures this is sensitive data processed abroad without consent (s.49(1)). (c) Reg. 26: the local pipeline keeps the serving copy in Kenya (limb (b)), but if the strict reading reported for the 2026 guidance is right, the processing itself must happen in Kenya. (d) No processor contract is modelled anywhere. (e) The "local" test is a hostname list, so a lawful Kenya-hosted endpoint cannot be used for held pages without `--allow-remote-unredacted`, which also opens the door to any foreign host.

**Options.**

*A1 — Keep the design; document the transfer.* Redactor: add `settings.transfer` *(new)* to the manifest with `{recipient, country, legal_basis: "s.48(c)(iii)|s.48(c)(vi)|safeguards", justification, dpa_reference}` taken from a `--transfer-record FILE` *(new)* that the operator writes once; `vlm_review` and `orientation` entries get `transfer_id` pointing at it. Harness: a `transfers.jsonl` *(new)* per job listing every image hash sent, the recipient and the basis, exportable as the reg. 41(2) record; `report.md` gets a "Transfers" section. Trade-offs: least effort; keeps the throughput the design relies on; **does not cure s.49(1)** for held pages, and leaves reg. 26 limb (a) unmet. Legal risk high for held pages, moderate for redacted ones.

*A2 — Region-aware guard: three tiers instead of two.* Redactor: replace the `LOCAL_HOSTS` boolean with a host classification `local | kenya | foreign` *(new)* driven by `--vlm-host-region KE|FOREIGN` *(new)* (or a `[hosts]` section in a config file, since a URL cannot prove its geography). Rules in code: unredacted pages (`--vlm-fallback`, `propose`, harness `held_view`) are allowed to `local` and `kenya` without any flag; to `foreign` only with `--allow-remote-unredacted` **and** a `--transfer-record`; redacted pages (`--vlm-audit`, `--orient`) to `foreign` only with a `--transfer-record`. Manifest: `vlm_review.host_region` *(new)* and `settings.transfer` as in A1. Harness: `held_pages: kenya` *(new value)* becomes the default; `held_pages: cloud` requires `transfer_record:` in the job file and the gate refuses release without it. Trade-offs: moderate effort (vlm.rs guard, main.rs flags, process.rs manifest, HARNESS §2, §4.7, §7, §9); unblocks a Kenya-hosted GPU (the design's own answer to the 12-concurrent-request ceiling); the region label is operator-asserted, so a false label is a compliance failure the manifest makes visible, not one the code can prevent. Satisfies reg. 26 limb (a) for anything routed to `kenya`.

*A3 — Blocking default: no personal data leaves Kenya unless explicitly released.* As A2, plus: the default host when `OLLAMA_API_KEY` is set is no longer `https://ollama.com`; `--vlm-audit` and `--orient` without a `kenya` or `local` host fail with a message naming reg. 26 and s.48, unless `--transfer-record` is given; harness `held_pages` has no `cloud` value at all, only `local | kenya | none`, and a job that needs a foreign model for held pages is refused at compile time (the §3 policy compiler reports "more than the policy allows"). Trade-offs: strongest legal position (reg. 26 limb (a) met for everything; s.49(1) never engaged); costs the owner's harness decision on held pages and makes Ollama Cloud an opt-in for redacted pages with paperwork; a Kenya-hosted inference endpoint for `gemma4:31b`-class vision models has to exist or be built (the DESIGN "at scale" section already describes the local-GPU route at about 5 core-seconds a form).

*A4 — Split by sensitivity: cloud for redacted pages, Kenya or local for held pages.* A2's classification; policy fixed in code: `held_view` and `locate` never go to `foreign` (the flag `--allow-remote-unredacted` is kept only for `propose`, which works on a reference image with handwriting already erased); `--vlm-audit` and `--orient` may go to `foreign` with a transfer record. Harness: `held_pages: cloud` removed; `held_pages: kenya|local|none`. Trade-offs: cures the s.49(1) problem outright; leaves the lower-risk s.48 transfer of redacted pages available where no Kenyan host exists; moderate effort; the orient and audit traffic still needs the reg. 41(2) record and, if the 2026 guidance is as reported, an ODPC notification.

**Recommendation: A4 now, with A3's blocking default as the target once a Kenya-hosted vision endpoint is available.** The held-page transfer is the one path where the law may be *categorically* against the design (s.49(1) consent, which cannot be obtained; VERIFIED text, with the premise that signatures are biometric only PARTIALLY supported, area 2.2). Nothing in the harness design depends on the held-page model being abroad: §7 already says "If the held-page role names a local host, nothing about held pages leaves the machine." Redacted-page transfers are defensible under s.48(c)(iii) or (vi) with documentation, so A4 keeps the throughput the owner wants while the reg. 26 question is confirmed. What would confirm or change this: the text of the ODPC Cross-border Guidance Notes 2026 on reg. 26 and on notification; the *Gachoka* court's treatment of reg. 26 and Safaricom-hosted IEBC systems; whether the IEBC results system is gazetted CII. (Reg. 2's silence on "strategic interest" is now CONFIRMED and no longer open.)

**Model hosting, specifically.** Three choices are in play: *local* (same machine; `LOCAL_HOSTS`), *Kenya-hosted* (a LAN or Kenyan data-centre endpoint, which today the code calls remote), *foreign cloud* (Ollama Cloud). The law distinguishes Kenya/abroad, not machine/network; the code distinguishes machine/network, not Kenya/abroad. Whatever option is chosen, the host classification should follow the law's line. The redactor's defaults should be: no `OLLAMA_API_KEY` → local; `OLLAMA_API_KEY` set → still refuse unredacted pages and require a transfer record for redacted ones (A4), or refuse everything foreign unless configured (A3).

### 2.2 Signatures: biometric or sensitive data? And the band treatment

**Requirement and risk.**
- DPA s.2 defines biometric data as "personal data resulting from specific technical processing based on physical, physiological or behavioural characterisation including …" (VERIFIED). The list is non-exhaustive; a signature is a behavioural characterisation; but an *image* of a signature on a form only becomes "biometric data" if it "results from specific technical processing" (A §3.2: "borderline; a signature-recognition model run over it is not"). Biometric data is sensitive personal data (s.2, VERIFIED), which engages s.44–45 (narrow grounds), s.49(1) (consent for processing abroad) and reg. 49(1)(c) (DPIA trigger).
- Two electoral instruments call signatures biometric: Elections (Technology) Regulations 2017 reg. 2, "'biometric' means unique identifiers or attributes including fingerprints, hand geometry, earlobe geometry, retina and iris patterns, voice waves, DNA, and signatures" (CONFIRMED verbatim from the Gazette copy, A-verification B2); the identical definition in Elections Act s.2, inserted by Act No. 36 of 2016 (B: [PRIMARY], Rev. 2019 edition). Those definitions govern their own instruments, not the DPA (the Technology Regulations say "in these Regulations"), but a court asked whether a signature image is biometric under the DPA's open list will see them, and the verification pass strengthens this area: both electoral definitions are now primary text.
- CMCA s.29 criminalises fraudulent use of "the electronic signature, password or any other unique identification feature of any other person" (VERIFIED). A reproducible signature image is a forgery exemplar.
- Electoral side: the presiding officer's signature is mandatory (reg. 79(1) [PRIMARY]); agents' refusal or absence is recorded and is not by itself fatal (reg. 79(3)–(7) [PRIMARY]; *Kaloki* [2018] KECA 555 STILL UNVERIFIED; second pass: CONFIRMED [PRIMARY] — "The agents' signature on the statutory forms are not mandatory. However, the signature of the presiding officer is mandatory"); the 2017 Registrar counted unsigned forms ("269 were original copies that were not signed", para 350) and the majority called the officer's signature "the last solemn act of assurance to the voter by such officer, that he stands by the 'numbers' on that form" (para 377) [PRIMARY]. Whether a line is signed is legally meaningful; what the strokes look like is not something any court has read off a portal image (B §8.3(1)).
- No Kenyan case on publishing signatures was found (A §7(12)).

**Today.** Each signature gets a black band through its middle, half its height; tops and bottoms of strokes show. In a table that did not snap, ink is covered in 8 px slices. Signatures in the comments and on forms read without a template are hidden whole (the box of the writing). A coverage check requires 90% of the ink inside the band to be black. There is no `--full-signatures`. The manifest records the policy (`signatures: band` is the harness's only value).

**Gap.** The band is a sound Art. 24(1)(e) answer: it keeps the legally meaningful fact (signed / not signed) and removes most of the stroke pattern. Residual exposure is that the top and bottom halves of the strokes remain and could, for a distinctive signature, still serve as a partial exemplar. Whether that residue is "biometric data" is exactly the borderline A §3.2 describes. The DPIA (area 2.8) should record the reasoning either way. The classification matters most for area 2.1 (s.49(1)) and the DPIA trigger, not for the band itself.

**Options.**

*S1 — Keep the band; record the classification.* Redactor: no change to masking; add `settings.signature_treatment: "band"` *(new)* explicitly to the manifest, and a `--signature-policy band|whole` flag reserved for S3. DPIA text: signatures treated as personal data and *assumed* sensitive for the purpose of ss.44–49 (conservative), while the band is justified under Art. 24(1)(e) and reg. 79(1). Effort: trivial. Transparency: unchanged (readers see the form was signed).

*S2 — Thicker band by default.* Redactor: raise the band from half the signature's height to two thirds (`masking.rs`), keeping the 2 px overrun and the 10 px minimum; the coverage check unchanged. Trade-offs: less residual stroke pattern; a compact signature's visible remnant shrinks toward "something was written here", which is still what reg. 79(1) needs; slightly more risk of covering a date written tight under a signature (DESIGN notes the band avoids the date line). Small effort; thresholds re-measured on the 215 Forms 34A.

*S3 — `--full-signatures`: hide whole.* Redactor: a flag that hides each signature's full ink box (as the comments path already does), recorded in `settings.full_signatures` *(new)* and replayed by `verify --rerun`; the VLM audit told that any signature stroke is a leak. Harness: `signatures: band|whole` in the policy. Trade-offs: removes the biometric question for published pages; **but** a fully blacked box no longer shows whether the line was signed or merely written over, which B §8.3(1) says not to do ("do not offer a 'full black-out' of signatures that would hide whether a line is signed at all"). Weakens the Art. 86 transparency story and the answer to the Kura Yangu Sauti Yangu complaint of 10 August 2017 about forms "neither signed nor stamped" (CORRECTED date; it concerned 2017, not 2022).

*S4 — Band plus a per-line "signed" attestation in the manifest.* As S1 or S2, plus the manifest records per signature line `signed: true|false|refused` *(new)* from the coverage pass (ink found or not), so a reader of a thick band or of a rescued page still gets the legally meaningful fact in a signed record; the overlay labels each band. Trade-offs: adds a machine-readable statement of fact the tool derives from ink detection, which can be wrong (faint strokes); mark it as `detected`, not asserted. Moderate effort.

**Recommendation: S1 now; S2 if the DPIA concludes the residual strokes are biometric; never S3 as a default.** The band already satisfies the courts' tests (presence or absence) and the minimisation principle; the open question is classification, which is a DPIA finding and a transfer constraint (area 2.1), not a masking change. What would confirm it: an ODPC view (the AI or electoral guidance, or a consultation under s.31(3)) on whether a signature image on a scanned form is "biometric data" under s.2.

### 2.3 ID, passport and phone numbers: are the partial-masking defaults enough?

**Requirement and risk.**
- An "identification number" is the paradigm identifier in the s.2 definition of "identifiable natural person" (VERIFIED); the ATI Act's "personal information" expressly includes "(c) any identifying number … (d) … telephone or other contact details" (VERIFIED). s.2 defines "anonymisation" as removal of identifiers "so that the data subject is no longer identifiable" and "pseudonymisation" as processing so that data "can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information" (VERIFIED). A number with 3 of 8 digits hidden beside a legible name is neither; it is partially masked personal data.
- **Reg. 37(1)(a)** deems a breach to carry a "real risk of harm" where it relates to "the data subject's full name … or identification number and any of the personal data or classes of personal data … set out in the Second Schedule" (CONFIRMED, A-verification E1; A's reconstruction was right). The regulator has singled out ID numbers as the identifier whose exposure matters. (Corrected, second pass: only **together with** Second Schedule data — pay, card and account numbers, debts, health and similar — none of which is on a result form; an ID number alone is outside the deemed class.) But reg. 37(3)(a) excludes "personal data that is publicly available", and the IEBC itself publishes the same ID numbers on its live portal (area 2.11).
- ***ODM v IEBC* (Kibra) [2019] KEHC 2492 cuts against masking, not for it (CORRECTED, A-verification C1).** The IEBC released the register with "identification numbers (truncated)" to protect confidentiality; the High Court held the IEBC "has not demonstrated how Article 31 of the Constitution of Kenya is" infringed, found that "for free and fair election to be had, national I/D or passport numbers provide" the needed particulars, and compelled publication "containing the names, National ID and Passport numbers". Two things survive for this area: the IEBC's own instinct was to truncate identifiers in an electoral record (the practice this tool generalises); and a party or candidate asking the IEBC for the unredacted forms under Art. 35 may succeed as ODM did. **(corrected, second pass, A-verification C1):** the court withheld **telephone numbers** from the published register: order (b) required publication "save confidential information and which may be injurious to voters such as telephone numbers … which is protected under Article 31", and the mandamus listed names, ID/passport numbers and polling stations only. Kibra is adverse on IDs and supportive on phones. The redacted copy cannot pre-empt that; it only declines to be a second public source.
- Phones: KICA s.27A(2)(c) makes SIM registration details confidential (VERIFIED); a published number is a key to a real-name identity. *Odhiambo & another v Attorney General & another* [2026] KEHC 3809 (19 March 2026) holds that a "phone number forms part of his/her digital identity just like the physical national" ID (citation CONFIRMED; snippet read; full holding not read). (Corrected, second pass: those words are the petitioners' prayer; the court's declaration, para 95(2), is that a "registered mobile phone number constitutes a digital identifier linking personal data that relates to an individual's private affairs hence qualifies for protection under Article 31 (c) & (d) of the Constitution" — a stronger footing for masking phones.) CMCA s.27 (cyber harassment) is the downstream harm (VERIFIED). Note that Kenya Law lists LN 90 of 2025 (Registration of Telecommunications Service Subscribers Regulations), which may have replaced the 2015 SIM Regulations (not checked). (Second pass: it did — LN 90 of 2025 reg. 22 revokes LN 163 of 2015; reg. 19(1) requires operators to keep subscribers' registration particulars secure and confidential "in accordance with the Act and the Data Protection Act".)
- Closest ODPC authority on publishing an ID number online: *Bliss Park Hotel* (Complaint 0778 of 2025), deletion ordered (STILL UNVERIFIED: tracker only; no hit on Kenya Law or in the press; ODPC site down). The IMEI case [2025] KEHC 10568: an identifier "became personal data as soon as it was associated with a person" (PARTIALLY VERIFIED; not re-checked; second pass: CONFIRMED, notices quashed).
- Electoral side (CORRECTED, B-verification §1.1): the "ID/Passport No." and "Tel. Contact" columns **are prescribed** for agents and candidates on Forms 34A, 34B and 34C by the Schedule substituted by LN 72/2017 r.44, and Forms 34B/34C prescribe the returning officer's and chairperson's "ID Number" [PRIMARY]. The IEBC publishes them unredacted on `forms.iebc.or.ke` (inspected 31 October 2026) [PRIMARY]. What remains true: no provision requires their *publication* as such (s.39(1C)(c) says "publish the polling result forms"); reg. 74 lodges the agent's name and address privately [PRIMARY]; no court has used an ID or phone number to verify a form. B §8.3(3), as corrected: the redaction question "turns on data-protection law and Articles 24 and 31(c), not on whether the fields are 'part of the form': they are", and from the electoral side "nothing favours showing them at all".

**Today.** Default `--hide 3`: IDs lose 3 random characters (never more than two neighbours left visible together); phones keep the network prefix and lose 3 of the last 6 digits (`--phone-tail 6`). `--full-ids` and `--full-phones` hide every character; both are recorded in `settings` and replayed by `verify --rerun`; the leak check tightens with them (3 written characters in a row outside a stamp hold the page). DESIGN "Known limits": "Hiding 3 digits of an 8-digit ID, or 3 of a phone number, leaves 1000 possible values. That is enough against casual reading, not against someone able to test candidates." Writing under a protected stamp stays visible (area 2.6). The harness compiles `ids: three` and `phones: tail` as defaults.

**Gap.** Partial masking is a stated design choice, not a legal requirement in either direction. Nothing in A or B gives a *data-protection* reason to publish any digit; the *electoral* argument for publishing them is that they are on the prescribed form and the IEBC publishes them, and Kibra shows a court ordering identifiers released when a party needed them. For a republisher, though, the s.25(d)/s.41(3) test is "necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed", and the republisher's purpose (verifiable results, accountability of named officials and agents) does not need the numbers. Against partial masking specifically: the 1,000-candidate residue combined with a visible name and polling station is a realistic re-identification risk (an ID number is checkable against KYC and loan-app services; a phone number against the recipient's own handset), so 3-of-8 masking neither meets minimisation nor matches the prescribed form; it is the one position with no legal argument behind it. The visible prefix on phones is low-risk on its own. The flags exist; only the defaults and the harness policy need to move. **Plainly: an Art. 35 request for the unredacted forms may succeed, and the IEBC's portal already shows them; the operator's masking is its own compliance choice, not a bar on anyone else's access.**

**Options.**

*N1 — Make `--full-ids` the default for agents and officers; keep phone tail.* Redactor: default `full_ids: true`; add `--partial-ids` *(new)* to restore the 3-character rule for operators who want digits visible for a documented reason; manifest unchanged in shape (`settings.full_ids` already exists); README/DESIGN tables updated. Harness: policy default `ids: whole`; "Standard redaction" compiles to it. Trade-offs: closes the reg. 37 exposure for the identifier the regulator cares about most; costs nothing in verifiability (no court reads IDs); the leak check already handles the mode; effort small. Phones stay as a 1,000-candidate pseudonym with the prefix visible.

*N2 — Full IDs and full phones by default.* As N1 plus `full_phones: true` by default, `--partial-phones` *(new)* to opt back. Trade-offs: the strongest minimisation; KICA/harassment risk closed; loses the (weak) argument that a visible prefix helps a party verify "that was our agent's number"; agents hold their own carbon copies (reg. 79(2A)(c)) so verification of their own entry does not depend on the public copy. Effort small.

*N3 — Keep partial defaults but hide more.* Redactor: default `--hide 5` for IDs (3 visible of 8, 100,000 candidates) and `--hide 4` of the last 6 for phones; rule "never more than one neighbouring character visible". Trade-offs: a half-measure; still neither anonymised nor pseudonymised in the s.2 sense; more complexity in the leak-check thresholds; hard to justify in a DPIA next to the free option of hiding the whole number.

*N4 — Keep today's defaults; shift the burden to the DPIA.* No code change; the DPIA argues that 3-of-8 masking with the name visible is "adequate" minimisation. Trade-offs: zero effort; weakest position; contradicts the tool's own documented limit; the harness's default policy would be publishing 50,000 forms with 1,000-candidate IDs.

**Recommendation: N2** (full IDs and full phones by default), with N1 as the minimum acceptable. Both reports converge: A §9.2 ("the `--full-ids` mode is the safer default for the agents' table … consider `--full-phones` for agents"), B §8.3(3) ("`--full-ids`/`--full-phones` is the safer default from this track's standpoint"). The prefix argument for phones is thin and the KICA link makes a Kenyan phone number a stronger identifier than in most jurisdictions. Partial masking should remain available under explicit flags and be recorded. After verification the recommendation stands but its footing has changed: it rests on DPA ss.25(d), 41(3) and 51(1) (VERIFIED), ATI s.6(1)(d) (VERIFIED) and Art. 24(1)(e) [PRIMARY], and on reg. 37 (CONFIRMED); it no longer claims Kibra, which is adverse for IDs (second pass: Kibra and *Odhiambo* para 95(2) now support the phone half of N2; reg. 37(1)(a) reaches ID numbers only together with Second Schedule data, so its support is indirect — it shows which identifiers the regulator treats as keys to harm, not a rule that a bare ID leak is notifiable). The priority stays **must** because the live alternative to N2 is not "publish as the IEBC does" (the tool exists to avoid that) but partial masking, which satisfies neither the DPA nor the prescribed form. If the IEBC itself is the controller (area 2.9), counsel should weigh whether s.39(1C)(c) read with the prescribed Schedule obliges the Commission to publish the identifiers; s.27(6) of its own Act and s.51(1) argue not.

### 2.4 Agents' names and party affiliation

**Requirement and risk.**
- s.2 "sensitive personal data" lists "race, health status, ethnic social origin, conscience, belief, genetic data, biometric data, property details, marital status, family details …, sex or the sexual orientation" and **not** political opinion or affiliation (VERIFIED). Party agency is therefore ordinary personal data today. A September 2026 ODPC **draft** notice under s.47 reportedly proposes "political affiliation and trade union membership" as sensitive, "only for data arriving by cross-border transfer from a jurisdiction whose own law already treats them as sensitive" (existence and draft status CONFIRMED: the ODPC page title is indexed and *The Standard*, 1 Oct 2026, reports comments open to 25 September 2026 and that "political affiliation includes information from which political leanings can reasonably be inferred"; the text itself is STILL UNVERIFIED and no final or gazetted notice was found, A-verification B4).
- The lawful basis for publishing names and party is the legal obligation in s.39(1C)(c) (s.30(1)(b)(ii)) and public task ((iv)/(v)) (VERIFIED). Names and signatures are the declaration's content on the prescribed form (Form 34 Schedule [PRIMARY]; reg. 79(1) [PRIMARY]); names serve the accountability function of Art. 81(e)(v) and 86 (B §8.3(2)). The IEBC's own privacy notice lists "Political Data: Information on political candidates, party agents, and representatives as required by law" [PRIMARY, read in full; it says nothing about the portal or redaction]. The names and party column are prescribed fields of Form 34A (LN 72/2017 Schedule [PRIMARY]).
- s.45(b) permits processing sensitive data "manifestly made public by the data subject" (VERIFIED): an agent who signs a public declaration as a party's agent has arguably done so, which would cover the case even if the s.47 notice is finalised in a broader form.
- "Reasons for refusal to sign" and officers' "statutory comments" are free text, legally meaningful (reg. 79(3)–(4) [PRIMARY]) and must stay readable (B §8.3(6)).

**Today.** Names and party names are never masked; the harness treats "Also hide the agents' names" as "more than the redactor can do" and refuses to release. The comments are OCR'd only to catch numbers and signatures; six comment-word fragments on five pages were masked as collateral (DESIGN "Comments on a templated form").

**Gap.** None on current law. Two watch items: the s.47 draft (if finalised without the cross-border limitation, the name+party pairing becomes sensitive data and the s.49(1) transfer question of area 2.1 widens to redacted pages); and collateral masking of comment words, which is a transparency cost rather than a privacy one.

**Options.**

*P1 — No change; register the watch item.* DPIA lists the s.47 draft as a monitored development; README states why names and party stay visible (s.39(1C)(c), reg. 79, Art. 86). Effort nil.

*P2 — Reserve a `names` policy in the harness and a `--mask-names` *(new)* zone action in the redactor, off and refused by default.* The template schema gets `action: name` zones on the agents' name column; the redactor refuses the flag unless `--i-understand-names-are-required` *(new)*, and the harness compiler maps "hide names" to it only when the job's `legal_basis` field says the publisher is not discharging s.39 (a third-party researcher under s.53, say). Trade-offs: readiness if the s.47 notice lands broad; but it creates a published-form variant that fails the accountability function and would need its own DPIA; moderate effort for a hedge against a draft whose text is unread and whose reported scope is limited to inbound cross-border data.

*P3 — Tighten the comments reader to stop masking words.* Redactor: in the free-text path, a masked piece must be a signature by the stricter rule *and* not overlap a line OCR read as capitals with fair confidence; log collateral word masks as `notes.collateral_text` *(new)* so the harness's risk signals can count them. Trade-offs: better transparency on the "reasons for refusal" field the courts care about; risk of leaving a signature that looks like a word; small effort; measurable on the 215-form set (currently 6 collateral pieces on 5 pages).

**Recommendation: P1 plus P3.** Names and party must stay visible; the law is clear and both reports agree. P2 is premature: the draft exists (CONFIRMED) but its text is unread and its reported scope is inbound cross-border data only. P3 is worth doing because the comments are the one place where the tool currently removes legally meaningful text. What would change this: the final text of the s.47 notice (odpc.go.ke).

### 2.5 Officials compared with agents

**Requirement and risk.**
- There is no general "public official" carve-out in the DPA (VERIFIED absence); the hooks are s.30(1)(b)(ii), (iv)–(vi), s.45(b), s.51(2)(c) and ATI s.6(1)(d)/(4)/(6) (A §1(5)). *Peter M. Kariuki v Attorney General* has been located on Kenya Law as [2011] KEHC 1564 (salaries and allowances of Major Generals; the State pleaded confidentiality) but its holding was not displayed (partly confirmed, A-verification C7); no Kenyan decision adopting a reduced-privacy doctrine for officials' *identifiers* was found (A §7(13)). The ODPC reportedly cautioned in 2026 against circulating MPs' phone numbers (STILL UNVERIFIED).
- Presiding and returning officers are election officials of a constitutional commission performing public functions; their names and signatures are the accountability mechanism (Art. 81(e)(v), 86(a); reg. 5(1), 79(1), 83(1)(d) [PRIMARY]). Agents are private persons appointed by parties (Elections Act s.30 [EXTRACT]; reg. 74 [PRIMARY]). Whether temporary polling officials are "public officers" under Art. 260 is UNVERIFIED and undecided (B §6).
- Officers stamp over their own details: in 2013, about 250 of 2,968 published pages had half or more of an officer's ID line inside a stamp (DESIGN); the harness counts 560 of 6,608 (HARNESS §8).
- Forms 34B and 34C prescribe the constituency returning officer's and the Commission chairperson's "ID Number", signature and date (LN 72/2017 Schedule [PRIMARY, CORRECTED]); Form 34A has no officer ID field.

**Today.** The redactor treats officers and agents identically: the same rules for IDs, phones and signatures; names visible for both. The ATI public-interest factors (s.6(6)(a), (e): accountability of public entities; whether a statutory authority is discharging its functions) are stronger for officers' *names and roles* than for agents', and both are already visible.

**Gap.** None that calls for weaker protection of officers' identifiers: A §9.7 and B §6 both conclude officials' ID numbers, phone numbers and signatures do not lose protection. There is a modest argument the other way for *agents*: their *affiliation* is more sensitive than an officer's role (area 2.4), and they are private persons, so if anything the agents' table deserves the stricter settings.

**Options.**

*O1 — Identical treatment (today), stated.* DPIA records that officers' identifiers are protected like agents', citing the absence of any Kenyan authority to the contrary and the s.6(1)(d) exemption. Effort nil.

*O2 — Per-role settings.* Redactor: zones carry `role: officer|agent` *(new)*; `--full-ids` / `--full-phones` can be set per role (`--full-ids agents`); manifest `settings.by_role` *(new)*. Harness: policy fields `ids_agents`, `ids_officers`. Trade-offs: lets an operator keep officers' digits partially visible for a "public role" reason; more settings to explain in a DPIA and to the VLM audit; no legal authority supports the asymmetry; moderate effort.

*O3 — Stricter for agents only.* As O2 but the only permitted asymmetry is *more* masking for agents (full IDs/phones for agents, partial for officers), never less. Trade-offs: aligns with the private-person status of agents; if area 2.3's N2 is adopted the asymmetry is moot.

**Recommendation: O1**, made moot by N2. Role tagging of zones (`role:`) is still worth adding for the *record* (the DPIA, the risk signals, and the s.43 notification, which must identify affected data subjects by class), but not for different masking.

### 2.6 Stamps: the authenticity role courts give them, against `--mask-stamps`

**Requirement and risk.**
- **The stamp has no footing in the Act, the Regulations or the prescribed form (CORRECTED).** Forms 34A and 34B as prescribed by LN 72/2017 have no stamp field (only Form 34D, the President-elect's certificate, has one) [PRIMARY]. The only instruments requiring it are Gazette Notice 9280 of 3 August 2022 ("ensure that the original Form 34A is appropriately signed and stamped") and Gazette Notices 9538–9540 of 8 August 2022 (KIEMS step 5 "Signs, dates and stamps the original and carbonated copies … with the provided Presiding Officer's Stamp"; step 14(c) confirm "the Form 34A has been stamped") [PRIMARY], administrative directions under reg. 82(1) and s.39. The 2017 Supreme Court recorded the respondents' contention that "official stamps were not a legal requirement" (para 336) and that "the only requirement under Regulations 79(2)(a) and 83(a) is for the signing of the forms" (para 354) without contradicting it [PRIMARY]. The sentence earlier attributed to the IEBC's deponent does not appear in the judgment and is withdrawn. Election Petition Appeal 3 of 2018 ("lack of it does not invalidate the Form") is STILL UNVERIFIED (second pass: *Ojuok v Okal & 2 others* [2018] KEHC 3736 (KLR), 12 July 2018, para 24: "lack of the official rubber stamp does not invalidate the Form or the results thereto" [PRIMARY]; the same in *Akumu v IEBC* [2018] KEHC 8842). The Kura Yangu Sauti Yangu complaint about forms "neither signed nor stamped" dates from 10 August 2017, not 2022 (CORRECTED).
- **Its evidentiary role is nevertheless confirmed at primary level.** The 2017 Registrar reported forms with "no evidence of being stamped or signed" (para 343); the majority's recital counted "26 of the Forms were stamped and scanned. 1 form was scanned and not stamped" and, inconsistently, "269 … not stamped" (paras 350, 368); and the Court asked whether forms lacking security features could "be still considered genuine" (para 376) [PRIMARY]. The 2022 Gazette procedure made the stamp part of the KIEMS checklist that the 2022 Court relied on ("consistent KIEMS reporting from verification to transmission", para 20(e)) [PRIMARY]. B §8.3(4), as corrected, still concludes that keeping stamps pixel-identical "is well aligned" and that `--mask-stamps` "removes an authenticity indicator the courts and civil society rely on".
- Against that, writing under a stamp is still an ID number, and s.25(d)/s.41(3) do not stop at the ring. A §9.2: "`--mask-stamps` is the minimisation-compliant default where the ID is legible. Record the choice in the DPIA."

**Today.** Default: no blacked block may touch a stamp; writing under one stays visible; the page notes record how much was lifted; `verify` checks stamps are untouched and the overlay outlines them. `--mask-stamps`: no stamp is protected; each field's band across the stamp is blacked whole (the ring and lettering off the fields' lines stay visible); recorded in `settings.mask_stamps`; stamps are then not listed as protected regions, so `verify` does not check them. The VLM audit reports `stamp_covered` as a failure in default mode. The 8 spots on stamps the review pointed at on the 215 forms were left visible.

**Gap.** The two reports pull in opposite directions and the current binary flag forces a whole-run choice: either every stamp is sacred (and a fully legible officer's ID under a stamp is published on hundreds of pages) or no stamp is protected (and `verify` stops vouching for any stamp). Neither is the proportionate answer. After verification the balance tilts: the stamp is an evidentiary convenience created by a 2022 Gazette direction, not a legal requirement, while the ID number under it is protected by VERIFIED statute (s.25(d), s.41(3)) and its exposure is a deemed-harm class (reg. 37, CONFIRMED). A design that sacrifices the identifier to preserve every pixel of a non-statutory mark is hard to defend in a DPIA; a design that blacks out the whole stamp throws away a mark the 2017 Court plainly weighed. Keeping the ring and lettering while masking the band under it is the answer to both. Note also that `verify`'s stamp check is dropped wholesale under `--mask-stamps`, which is a transparency regression: the stamp's *ring* is still untouched and could still be verified.

**Options.**

*T1 — Keep the default (stamps sacred); use `--mask-stamps` by exception.* DPIA documents the known residue (ID digits legible under stamps on a measurable share of 2013 pages; 8 spots on 215 Forms 34A). Harness: the risk signal "writing inside a stamp on an ID or signature line" already exists; add a report count. Trade-offs: maximal authenticity; a known, counted minimisation failure that the regulator would see as a choice, not an accident.

*T2 — Mask under stamps, but keep the ring verifiable.* Redactor: `--mask-stamps` changed so that stamps *stay listed* as protected regions with a new `kind: "stamp_ring"` *(new)*: the annulus (ring plus lettering outside the fields' bands) is protected and verified pixel-identical; only the field's band across the disc may be blacked. `verify` checks the annulus; the overlay outlines it. Manifest: `settings.mask_stamps: "bands"` *(new value)*. Trade-offs: closes the minimisation gap for identifiers under stamps while keeping the thing a court looks for (a ring with "PRESIDING OFFICER" lettering) intact and provable; moderate effort in `stamps.rs`, `pipeline.rs`, `verify.rs`; makes `--mask-stamps` safe enough to be the harness default.

*T3 — Conditional masking: only where digits are legible under the stamp.* Redactor: `--mask-stamps auto` *(new)*: the band across the stamp is blacked only when the ink under the stamp segments into 3 or more character slots that are not the stamp's own lettering (DESIGN says this is hard: the pen's hue lies within the stamp's spread). Trade-offs: least intrusive on stamps; relies on a classifier DESIGN already reports as unreliable; a miss publishes a legible ID; a false positive blackens part of a stamp for nothing.

*T4 — Mask under stamps for IDs and phones, never for signatures.* As T2, but signature bands across a stamp are not applied (a signature's residue under a stamp is a weaker exemplar than a full ID number is an identifier). Trade-offs: keeps more of the stamp intact on the Form 34A officers' lines, where the stamp most often sits over a signature; the ID-under-stamp cases (the 2013 problem) are still cured.

**Recommendation: T2, with T4's carve-out considered after measuring how often the band would cross the ring's lettering.** It is the only option that satisfies both reports' concerns at once: minimisation (A) and a verifiable authenticity mark (B). It should become the harness policy default (`stamps: bands`). After verification T1 (never mask under a stamp) is weaker than first assessed: it preserves a mark the law does not require at the cost of an identifier the law protects, so it is acceptable only as an explicit opt-in with the residue counted in every job report. T2 rests on VERIFIED minimisation text, CONFIRMED reg. 37 and [PRIMARY] Gazette and Supreme Court texts; the one remaining unverified authority (Election Petition Appeal 3 of 2018) would only bear on how much weight a lower court gives a missing stamp, which T2 does not create.

### 2.7 Automated decisions and the "no one approves a job's settings" rule

**Requirement and risk.**
- s.35: a data subject has the right not to be subject to a decision "based solely on automated processing … which produces legal effects concerning or significantly affects the data subject" (VERIFIED). A §3.7: a model deciding to mask or hold a page is not such a decision *about* the data subject, "but a wholly automated decision to *publish* a person's unredacted identifiers could be argued to 'significantly affect' them; keep a human-review path."
- Reg. 49(1)(a) makes "automated decision making with legal or similar significant effect" a DPIA trigger (VERIFIED). s.41(2): protection by design applies "at the time of the determination of the means of processing" (VERIFIED), which is what the harness's policy compiler does.
- Reg. 5(2)–(3): one legal basis, established before processing and "demonstrable at all times" (VERIFIED).

**Today (design).** The orchestrator model compiles free-text instructions into a closed policy; "No one approves the policy. It runs as compiled" (HARNESS §3, §13). Code enforces a floor (signatures always banded; names, results never masked) and a ceiling (instructions the redactor cannot meet stop release). The release gate is code. A person sees the policy only in the report, after the fact. Held pages that cannot be cleared go "to people, with a category and a suggested action" (§4.7).

**Gap.** The individual masking decisions are rule-based with a model as second pair of eyes, and the publish decision is a code gate: that is defensible. The exposed point is the *policy compilation*: a model's reading of free text sets `ids`, `phones`, `stamps` for the whole job with no human confirmation. If the compiler reads "keep the phone prefixes" as `phones: tail` when the requester meant something else, 50,000 forms publish under a setting nobody signed off. Under areas 2.3 and 2.6 the floor should rise so the compiler's degrees of freedom shrink; what remains is small but should still be visible to a person before release.

**Options.**

*D1 — Keep "no approval"; raise the floor.* Harness: with N2 and T2 adopted, the policy's only free fields are `uncertain_tables`, `vision_review`, `per_page` and the opt-outs (`partial_ids`, etc.), which can only *weaken* protection if the job file names a `legal_basis` and a `transfer_record`. The report still opens with the policy. Trade-offs: preserves the owner's decision; the compiler can no longer produce a less protective run by misreading text, because less protective settings need explicit structured fields, not prose.

*D2 — Policy acknowledgement, not approval.* Harness: phase 2 writes `plan.json` with the compiled policy and flags; the job does not *release* (phase 8) until the requester has acknowledged the policy by signing `plan.json` (`rfpii job ack` *(new)*), which the gate checks as condition 8. Redaction, QA and triage run unattended meanwhile. Trade-offs: a human sees the settings before anything is public, without blocking the long-running phases; small effort; costs a round-trip per job.

*D3 — Full approval before run.* The job waits after phase 2. Trade-offs: contradicts the owner's decision; no legal provision requires it.

**Recommendation: D1 plus D2.** The law does not require human approval of masking rules; it requires a demonstrable basis (reg. 5(3)) and no solely-automated decision with significant effect on a person. D2 gives the operator a signed record of who accepted which policy, which is also the evidence a s.43 notification or an ODPC audit will ask for, at the cost of one acknowledgement per job and no delay to the run.

### 2.8 Data protection impact assessment

**Requirement and risk.**
- s.31(1): a DPIA "prior to the processing" where processing "is likely to result in high risk" (VERIFIED). Reg. 49(1) deems high risk for "(b) use of personal data on a large-scale for a purpose other than that for which the data was initially collected", "(c) processing biometric or genetic data", "(e) processing sensitive personal data", "(g) large scale processing of personal data", "(i) innovative use or application of new technological or organizational solutions" (VERIFIED). The tool meets (b), (g) and (i) on any reading and (c)/(e) on the conservative reading of signatures.
- s.31(2) content: systematic description, necessity and proportionality, risks, measures (VERIFIED). s.31(3): consult the Commissioner if residual high risk. s.31(5): "The data impact assessment reports shall be submitted sixty days prior to the processing" (CONFIRMED verbatim). Reg. 50: Third Schedule template, with a field for "Safeguard prior to and Cross border transfers, if any". Reg. 52(3): silence for 60 days = deemed approval; reg. 52(4): the report may be published. Reg. 53: periodic audits against the report (all VERIFIED). ODPC DPIA Guidance Note, 28 Nov 2023 (STILL UNVERIFIED; ODPC site down).
- Courts have quashed processing started without a DPIA (both CONFIRMED, A-verification D3–D4): *Republic v Mucheru; ex parte Katiba Institute* [2021] KEHC 122 quashed the Huduma Card roll-out "for being ultra vires section 31 of the Data Protection Act" and issued "Mandamus compelling the respondents to conduct a data protection impact assessment"; *Worldcoin* [2025] KEHC 5629 found no DPIA "contrary to section 31" and ordered the data erased within 7 days under the Data Commissioner's supervision. Reg. 49 and s.31(5) are CONFIRMED verbatim (D1–D2).
- The reported AI Guidance draft (July 2026, UNVERIFIED) adds: DPIA before high-risk AI deployment; documented adequacy assessment for any offshore AI processor.

**Today.** No DPIA exists and the tool produces nothing aimed at one. The material a DPIA needs is scattered across DESIGN.md (masking per field, gates, residual risks, measured results), the manifests (settings, protected regions, VLM records) and HARNESS.md (privacy rules, scrubber, canary tests). The harness report (§10) is a job report, not a risk assessment.

**Gap.** The 60-day clock (s.31(5)) means a DPIA has to be filed about two months before a real run; for a 2027 general-election job that is a fixed date. The s.31(2)(a) "systematic description" and (d) "measures" can be generated from the code and the manifests; (b) necessity/proportionality and (c) risks need a human author, but the tool can supply the numbers (residual candidate set per masking mode, leak-rate bound, stamp residue count, transfer inventory). After verification the necessity section has a harder question to answer than first framed: the identifiers are prescribed fields of the statutory form (LN 72/2017, CORRECTED) and the IEBC publishes them unredacted on a live portal (CORRECTED), so the DPIA must explain why the *republisher's* purpose does not need them and why a second public source adds risk the first does not (permanence, indexability, bulk download), rather than argue that the fields should not be there.

**Options.**

*I1 — `rfpii dpia-annex` *(new)*: a generated technical annex.* From a set of manifests (or a pilot run) it writes a Markdown/JSON annex in the Third Schedule's headings: processing description (inputs, fields, treatments per role, hosts and regions used, prompts by hash), data categories and volumes, residual-risk figures (candidate-set size per mode, pages with writing under stamps, collateral text masks, leak-rate bound achieved), safeguards (block proof, gates, guard, scrubber, canary tests), retention settings (area 2.10), transfer records (area 2.1). Trade-offs: makes the DPIA's (a) and (d) sections reproducible and auditable (reg. 53); moderate effort; the human parts still have to be written.

*I2 — Harness phase 0: "DPIA present" as a gate condition.* Harness: the job file names `dpia: {reference, submitted_on, approved_on|deemed_on}` *(new)*; the release gate (§9) adds condition 9: a DPIA reference whose submission date is at least 60 days before the job, or a deemed-approval date, else the job runs to `staging/` and stops. The pilot (§4.3) is allowed without it (it is the pilot that produces the annex's numbers). Trade-offs: cheap; converts a legal deadline into a code check; could be bypassed by a fake reference, so it is a reminder with a record, not a proof.

*I3 — Publish the DPIA with the batch root.* `public/` carries the DPIA (or its annex) beside the ledger; reg. 52(4) permits publication; it answers the "doctored forms" narrative with the regulator-facing document. Trade-offs: transparency; small effort once I1 exists.

**Recommendation: I1 + I2 + I3.** A DPIA is mandatory on VERIFIED text; the only question is how much of it the software writes. The annex generator is the one piece another engineer can build without a lawyer. What would sharpen it: the ODPC DPIA Guidance Note's template fields and whether the electoral Guidance Note says anything about result forms (both STILL UNVERIFIED: the ODPC site was under maintenance on both passes; A §5.1 found no source reporting any such passage; the National Assembly asked the ODPC on 15 October 2026 to update the electoral note within six months, CONFIRMED).

### 2.9 Controller and processor roles, registration, records of processing and contracts

**Requirement and risk.**
- s.2: controller "determines the purpose and means of processing"; processor "processes personal data on behalf of the data controller" (VERIFIED). s.42(2): a controller using a processor must choose one with "sufficient guarantees" and enter "a written contract"; s.42(3): a processor processing "other than as instructed … shall be deemed to be a data controller in respect of that processing" (VERIFIED). Reg. 24(2) contract contents; reg. 25(1) sub-processor authorisation; reg. 21(4) data-sharing agreements for routine sharing; reg. 23(2)(e) a published data protection policy naming overseas recipients where possible (VERIFIED).
- Registration: reg. 4(1) of LN 265: every controller and processor registers; reg. 13(2) exempts turnover under KES 5m and under ten employees, but reg. 13(4) removes the exemption for Third Schedule purposes, which include "1. Canvassing political support among the electorate" (VERIFIED). The IEBC's registration should be treated as **incomplete**: on 15 October 2026 the National Assembly adopted the Constitutional Oversight Committee's report recommending that the "IEBC … expedite its registration and full compliance, including data processing and sharing agreements, before the 2027 General Election" (Hansard, CONFIRMED, A-verification F2); the ODPC register itself could not be read (STILL UNVERIFIED). A small third-party operator is probably exempt unless its purpose is read as canvassing (it is not; it is publication or research) — but the reported AI Guidance draft would require registration of entities deploying AI on Kenyans' data (STILL UNVERIFIED).
- A §9.1 on roles: **if run by or for the IEBC**, the IEBC is controller (basis s.30(1)(b)(ii) via s.39(1C)(c), and (iv)/(v)); the operator and any model host are processors needing s.42(2)(b) contracts; the processing is squarely reg. 26(2)(b). **If run by a third party** on IEBC-published forms, that party is the controller (basis s.30(1)(b)(vii) legitimate interests or (viii) journalistic/research; s.28(2)(a) permits indirect collection from "a public record"); the model host is its processor; reg. 26 may still bite.
- s.30(2): further processing "shall be in accordance with the purpose of collection" (VERIFIED). Sending redacted pages to a model is further processing, "defensible as quality-assurance of the publication, weaker for a cloud host abroad" (A §3.4).

**Today.** The tool has no notion of who the controller is. The manifest identifies the publisher only through the signing key (`key_id`) and the PDF certificate (`certgen --name "Returning Officer, Kibwezi West" --org ...`). Nothing records the legal basis, the controller, the processor list or a contract reference. The harness job file has `sign_key` and `models` but no `controller`, `legal_basis` or `processors`.

**Gap.** Reg. 5(3) ("demonstrable at all times") and reg. 41(2) both want a record that today lives nowhere. The controller/processor question also decides who files the DPIA, who notifies breaches (s.43(1) vs (3)), and whether the operator's own masking choices make it a controller under s.42(3) (an operator who decides to publish with `--partial-ids` against the IEBC's instruction has "determined the means").

**Options.**

*R1 — A `controller.json` *(new)* beside the ledger, referenced by every manifest.* Fields: controller name and ODPC registration number (or `registration_status: pending|exempt` with the ground; for the IEBC today, "pending", citing the 15 October 2026 National Assembly resolution), operator and its role (controller | processor for …), legal basis (s.30(1)(b)(…)) with one sentence of justification (reg. 5), processors (model hosts, with country, contract reference and sub-processor authorisation), DPO contact, retention schedule reference (area 2.10), DPIA reference (area 2.8). The manifest carries `controller_sha256` *(new)*; `verify` checks the file matches and prints the controller. Harness: the job file requires `controller:` and the policy compiler refuses to run without it. Trade-offs: cheap; makes reg. 5(3), reg. 23(2)(e) and reg. 41(2) demonstrable from the published set; the published file must not contain personal data beyond a DPO contact.

*R2 — Processor-mode guard.* Redactor: `--as-processor INSTRUCTIONS.json` *(new)*: the operator declares it is a processor and the file carries the controller's signed instruction (the settings it allows); the redactor refuses settings outside it (the way the harness refuses instructions below the floor). Trade-offs: gives the s.42(3) protection teeth (the operator cannot drift into controller status by accident); moderate effort; only meaningful in the IEBC-processor scenario.

*R3 — Nothing in the tool; keep records on paper.* Trade-offs: legal duty met if the paperwork exists; the published set does not show it; the harness's report cannot cite it.

**Recommendation: R1 for every run; R2 if the IEBC is the controller.** The IEBC-as-controller scenario is the one where reg. 26 clearly applies and the contract chain (IEBC → operator → model host → host's sub-processors) must exist on paper under s.42(2)(b)/reg. 24/reg. 25. The owner should decide which scenario the 2027 run is before building more of the harness, because it changes who signs the DPIA and who the gate's `transfer_record` must name. What would confirm: the IEBC's entry in the ODPC register once the site is back (Parliament's 15 October 2026 resolution implies there is none yet); reg. 13 as applied to the operator's turnover and headcount. An IEBC-commissioned run before the IEBC is registered would put the operator in a processor relationship with an unregistered controller, which the DPIA should name as a risk.

### 2.10 Retention: the portal (closed after 90 days in 2022, online again in 2026), the 3-year originals, and the tool's own copies

**Requirement and risk.**
- s.39(1): retain "only as long as may be reasonably necessary" unless "(a) required or authorised by law; (b) reasonably necessary for a lawful purpose; … (d) for historical, statistical, journalistic … or research purposes"; s.39(2): delete, anonymise or pseudonymise what need not be retained (VERIFIED). Reg. 19(2)–(3): a written retention schedule with purpose, period, periodic audit and post-audit actions (VERIFIED). Reg. 35(d): ensure anonymised data cannot be re-identified or deleted data recovered, "and testing whether this is possible"; reg. 35(f): decide what personal data and length of storage is necessary "for back-ups and logs" (VERIFIED). Reg. 24(2)(e): processor data "permanently deleted or returned on termination" (VERIFIED).
- Electoral side: reg. 93(1): all election documents retained by the returning officer for three years, then disposed of subject to the Public Archives and Documentation Service Act [PRIMARY]; Technology Regulations reg. 17: "All electronic data relating to an election shall be retained in safe custody by the Commission for a period of three years after the results of the elections have been declared" [PRIMARY, CONFIRMED verbatim]. *KHRC v Chebukati* (Mugambi J, 9 Jan 2025): closing the portal 90 days after the 2022 election was lawful; the portal is "for the transmission of election results and not as a long-term archive"; thereafter access is on request under the ATI Act [SECONDARY, *The Standard* 21 Jan 2025; citation and appeal STILL UNVERIFIED]. **But on 31 October 2026 `forms.iebc.or.ke` is online and serves the 2022 General Election forms again, unredacted, beside the 2022–2026 by-elections [PRIMARY, CORRECTED]**; when it re-opened is not stated and the Commission is under no duty to keep it up. **Today.** The redactor writes `public/` (redacted PDFs, manifests, ledger, batch roots: the manifests contain no PII), `review/` (JSON reports per withheld document, `report.jsonl`, and `review/vlm/` with VLM-redacted copies "for approval"). The originals are wherever the operator put them; the tool never deletes anything. The harness adds `staging/`, `quarantine/`, `defects/` (with "redacted evidence" crops), the journal (hashes, no images) and the held-page triage views that were *sent* (hashed, not stored, per §7). VLM crops are sent, not stored locally. There is no retention setting and no deletion step.

**Gap.** The operator will hold, for an indefinite time: the unredacted originals (needed for `verify` and for any dispute, so retention is "reasonably necessary for a lawful purpose" but needs a schedule); held pages in `review/` that still show PII; `review/vlm/` copies; quarantined pages. Reg. 35(d) asks for a *test* that the masked blocks cannot be recovered; the coefficient-level JPEG edit (black DC, zero AC, chroma neutral) is irreversible by construction, but that fact is asserted in DESIGN.md, not demonstrated as a test artefact.

**Options.**

*K1 — A retention schedule in code: `rfpii retain` *(new)*.* A `retention.yaml` *(new)* beside `controller.json` names, per folder class, the purpose, the period and the action: originals (e.g. until 3 years after declaration, mirroring reg. 93, or until the verification window the operator commits to), `review/` and `review/vlm/` (e.g. 90 days after the job's release, or on triage completion), `staging/`, `quarantine/`, `defects/` crops, `journal.jsonl` (indefinite: no PII), `public/` (indefinite). `rfpii retain --audit` lists what is past due; `--apply` deletes with a signed ledger entry recording what was deleted (hashes and counts, no content). Trade-offs: meets reg. 19(2)–(3) and reg. 35(f) directly; moderate effort; the originals' period is a policy decision for the controller (area 2.9), not the tool.

*K2 — Harness phases 8–9 end with disposal.* Harness: after release and report, `review/` held pages that triage classed "not a form for this job" or "to people" move to an encrypted `held/` archive with an expiry; everything else in `staging/` that was released is deleted (the copy in `public/` is canonical); `defects/` crops must be redacted crops only (already the design) and get an expiry. The report lists what was retained and until when. Trade-offs: small addition to the design; makes the job folder's end state defensible.

*K3 — Irreversibility test artefact.* Redactor: `verify` gains a `PASS  masked blocks hold no recoverable data (DC black, AC zero; chroma neutral)` line *(new)* that checks every listed block's coefficients are exactly the black constants (it already checks "listed and solid black"; this names the reg. 35(d) property and prints it); `tests/` gains a test that attempts recovery from the coefficients and fails. Trade-offs: trivial effort; gives the DPIA a cited test rather than a design statement.

*K4 — Nothing; document on paper.* Weakest; the folders accumulate PII with no owner.

**Recommendation: K1 + K2 + K3.** All three are small and together they turn s.39/reg. 19 from a paper schedule into something the job report can cite. The period for originals should be set by the controller after deciding the verification window the published set promises (area 2.13): the IEBC portal is online today but was closed for a period after 2022 and may close again, and if it does the operator's copy of the original may be the only one a verifier can get without an ATI request, which argues for keeping originals (encrypted, area 2.14) for the three-year reg. 93 period rather than deleting early. While the portal is up, `verify` users can fetch and hash the IEBC copy directly, and F3 (area 2.15) lets them match it.

### 2.11 Breach detection and notification (72 hours) in the post-release workflow

**Requirement and risk.**
- s.2: a "personal data breach" includes "unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data" (VERIFIED). A published page with an unmasked ID, phone or signature is one, and for ID numbers reg. 37(1)(a) deems a "real risk of harm" (CONFIRMED). **(corrected, second pass, A-verification E1):** reg. 37(1)(a) deems that risk only where the name or ID number comes "and any of the personal data … set out in the Second Schedule", a list of financial, health, child-protection and similar data that a result form does not carry. A form leak is therefore not a deemed notifiable breach; whether s.43(1)'s "real risk of harm" exists is an assessment, which the paragraph below already asks the operator to make and record. Exposure of ID and phone numbers to fraud, SIM-swap and harassment will often make the risk real, so notification will often still be due. Reg. 37(3)(a) excludes "personal data that is publicly available"; reg. 37(4) says data is not publicly available "solely because of any data breach" (VERIFIED). **After verification this exclusion matters more than first stated:** the IEBC's own portal is online on 31 October 2026 and shows the same agents' ID numbers, phone numbers and signatures in full (CORRECTED). While that is so, the identifiers *are* publicly available through a lawful publication, not "solely because of" the operator's leak, so the deemed-harm classification of reg. 37(1) may not attach and s.43(1)'s "real risk of harm" condition is arguable. That argument lapses the moment the portal closes again (as it did in 2022), and it does not make the leak lawful: it is still an unauthorised disclosure in the s.2 sense, a failure of the operator's own s.41 safeguards, and a reg. 37(3)(b) question (disclosure "to the extent that is required or permitted under any written law" does not describe a redaction failure). The operator should therefore run the s.43 assessment for every confirmed leak and record its conclusion, expecting notification to be required at least whenever the IEBC copy is not online. (Second pass: the reg. 37(3)–(4) "publicly available" debate above matters only for Second Schedule data, which forms do not carry; the live test is s.43(1) real risk of harm.)
- s.43(1)(a): notify the Data Commissioner "without delay, within seventy-two hours of becoming aware"; (1)(b): communicate to the data subject "within a reasonably practical period, unless the identity of the data subject cannot be established"; (2): a late notification carries reasons; (3): a processor notifies the controller "without delay and where reasonably practicable, within forty-eight hours"; (5) and reg. 38: contents (nature, chronology, how it occurred, number of data subjects, classes of data, potential harm, remedial action, communication to affected persons, DPO contact); (6): communication to data subjects not required where safeguards such as encryption were in place (not our case: the data was in clear on a public page); (8): record facts, effects and remedial action (all VERIFIED). s.62(2)(c), (h): mitigation and self-notification reduce penalties (VERIFIED). - "Becoming aware" is the trigger. In the harness, awareness is a confirmed finding in QA or, after release, a confirmed finding on a page already in `public/`.

**Today.** The redactor has no post-publication path at all: a leak found later is fixed by re-running with a new seed and republishing by hand (the 2013 experience). The harness design has: QA before release (§4.5), remediation by spot masks before release (§4.6), `quarantine/` for pages "withdrawn after QA" (§1 table), and a journal. It does **not** define: what happens when a leak is confirmed on a *released* page; who is notified; how the clock is recorded; or how the fix (a republished page with a new manifest) is tied to the leaked one in the ledger.

**Gap.** The design releases groups once they pass a 0.5% leak-rate bound. By construction up to 0.5% of released pages may carry a leak (for 50,000 forms, up to 250 pages). Some will be found later, by the operator's own sampling, by readers, or by the affected person. There is no incident workflow, so the 72-hour clock would start running against an operator with no tooling to establish the chronology (reg. 38(1)(b)) or the number of data subjects (reg. 38) quickly.

**Options.**

*B1 — A `rfpii withdraw` / republish pair *(new)* in the redactor.* `withdraw PAGE --reason` writes a signed ledger entry `{withdrawn: manifest_hash, at, reason_code}` and removes the PDF from `public/` (the manifest stays, so the ledger chain and batch root remain provable). The page is re-redacted with `--spot-masks` (already planned) or new settings and republished with a new manifest whose `supersedes` *(new)* field names the withdrawn one. `verify` reports a superseded manifest as such. Trade-offs: gives a provable chronology (the ledger is hash-chained and timestamped: the reg. 38(1)(b) "chronology" and s.43(8) record for free); small effort; does nothing about notification by itself.

*B2 — Harness phase 10 "Incident" *(new)*.* Triggered by a confirmed finding on a released page (from the operator's continued sampling, a `report-leak` tool *(new)* that accepts a page id from a reader, or a data subject's message). Code, not a model, then: records `aware_at`; withdraws the page (B1); computes the affected data subjects by class from the manifest's zone roles (area 2.5's `role:` tags: "1 agent, ID number and phone number", never the values); drafts the s.43(5)/reg. 38 notice from a template with the facts filled in (nature, chronology from the ledger, how it occurred from the page notes and held reason, number and classes, remedial action = withdrawal and republication, DPO contact from `controller.json`); starts a 72-hour timer and a 48-hour timer if the operator is a processor (area 2.9); writes everything to `incidents/` *(new)* and the journal. A person sends the notice; the harness records when. Trade-offs: moderate effort; turns the statutory deadlines into a tracked workflow; the notice to the data subject (s.43(1)(b)) still needs a human because the tool must not read the name off the form to address it (the scrubber would strip it) — the operator can, offline, from the original.

*B3 — Pattern escalation.* Harness: three confirmed post-release leaks of one kind in a group re-open the group (gate condition 5), quarantine every page with the shared signal, and open a defect; the incident record notes the broader class of potentially affected subjects. Trade-offs: the existing §4.6 pattern rule applied after release; small addition.

*B4 — Rely on the leak-rate bound and handle incidents ad hoc.* Trade-offs: nil effort; the 72-hour clock will be missed or met by luck; s.62(2)(h) credit lost.

**Recommendation: B1 + B2 + B3, with B1 a must and B2/B3 a should.** The harness design is incomplete against VERIFIED text: a 0.5% bound is a statement that leaks will occur, and s.43 says what then happens. B1 (withdraw and republish with a provable chronology) is needed whatever the notification analysis says, so it stays **must**. B2's timers and notice drafts drop to **should** because, after verification, whether a given leak is notifiable depends on whether the IEBC's own copy of the same identifiers is publicly available at the time (reg. 37(3)(a)): the incident record should therefore capture `iebc_portal_online_at_awareness: true|false` *(new)* and the operator's s.43(1) assessment, and the notice template should be ready to send rather than sent automatically. - Ollama Cloud's retention and training terms are not in either report and were not checked; the harness design notes the catalogue changes without notice ("several tags were retired in June 2026").

**Today.** The manifest records model, host, prompt hashes and image hashes. Nothing records the vendor's terms (retention, training use, sub-processors, region) at the time of the run, and the terms a hash was sent under can change between runs.

**Gap.** Even for *redacted* pages, a vendor that retains or trains on the images is a further processing the operator did not authorise (s.42(3) problem for the vendor, s.42(2)(a) "sufficient guarantees" problem for the operator). The operator cannot show reg. 24(2) terms exist.

**Options.**

*V1 — Terms snapshot in the transfer record.* Redactor: `--transfer-record` (area 2.1) includes `vendor_terms: {url, sha256, retrieved_at, retention, training_use, sub_processors, region}`; the manifest's `vlm_review` and `orientation` carry the transfer record id. Harness: at job start, code fetches and hashes the vendor's terms page and refuses to start if `training_use` is not "none" or `retention` exceeds the job's setting (values are operator-entered against the fetched text; code cannot parse legal terms). Trade-offs: small; gives the reg. 24 and AI-guidance paperwork a timestamped anchor.

*V2 — Zero-retention endpoints only.* Policy: foreign vendors are permitted only through an endpoint with contractual zero retention and no training, named in `controller.json`'s processor list. Trade-offs: may exclude Ollama Cloud's consumer tier; shifts the owner toward Kenya-hosted or local models (area 2.1 A3).

*V3 — Ignore; rely on the images being redacted.* Trade-offs: the names, party and stamps on redacted pages are still personal data; the reg. 24 duty is unconditional.

**Recommendation: V1, moving to V2 for anything but pilots.** The AI guidance is UNVERIFIED and still a draft, but the contract duty it restates is VERIFIED in s.42(2)(b) and reg. 24. What would confirm: Ollama Cloud's current terms and whether a business tier offers a data-processing agreement; the final AI Guidance Note text.

## 4. Open legal points for a Kenyan lawyer or verification against primary texts

Each point names the provision or source, why it matters here, and the area it would settle.

Points resolved by the 31 October 2026 verification pass are marked **resolved** and replaced by the live question that remains behind them.

1. **Reg. 26, LN 263 of 2021: scope and strictness.** (a) *Resolved:* reg. 2 defines neither "strategic interest of the state" nor "public good" (CONFIRMED). (b) Confirm whether a third party redacting IEBC-published forms is processing "for the purpose of … facilitating the conduct of elections" (reg. 26(2)(b)). (c) Read the ODPC Cross-border Guidance Notes 2026 (8 Sept 2026) on whether limb (b) "serving copy in Kenya" suffices for election data and whether transfers must be notified to the ODPC per transfer or on request (secondary accounts conflict). Settles whether area 2.1 should be A4 or A3, and whether redacted-page transfers need a notification step.
2. **Signature images as "biometric data" under DPA s.2.** Whether an image of a handwritten signature on a scanned form is "personal data resulting from specific technical processing based on … behavioural characterisation". The Elections (Technology) Regulations reg. 2 and Elections Act s.2 definitions (signatures listed; both now [PRIMARY]) are persuasive but govern their own instruments. Settles s.49(1) consent exposure (area 2.1), reg. 49(1)(c)/(e) DPIA triggers (2.8) and whether S2 is needed (2.2). An ODPC view via s.31(3) consultation would be the practical route.
3. **Reg. 37(3)(a) "publicly available" where the IEBC itself publishes the same identifiers.** *Reg. 37(1) text is resolved (CONFIRMED).* (Second pass: on the full text the deemed class needs Second Schedule data, which forms do not carry, so the deemed-harm question falls away; what remains is the s.43(1) real-risk assessment.) The live question: when an identifier leaks on the operator's redacted copy while the IEBC's portal shows it in full, is it "publicly available" so that the deemed-harm class does not attach, and is there a "real risk of harm" under s.43(1)? And does the answer change when the portal is down? Settles whether B2's notification is automatic or assessed (area 2.11).
4. ***ODM v IEBC* [2019] KEHC 2492 (Kibra): reach.** *The operative order is resolved (CORRECTED: register published with names and ID/passport numbers; A-verification C1 read the order in Kenya Law snippets; press reports still conflict).* The live questions: does the reasoning ("for free and fair election to be had, national I/D or passport numbers provide …") extend from a voters' register sought by a party to published result forms sought by a party, candidate or citizen under Art. 35; and what did the Supreme Court order in *Mue v IEBC* [2017] KESC 45 (reasoning partly recovered: "information held by the State or State organs, unless for very exceptional circumstances …"; orders STILL UNVERIFIED). (Second pass: *Mue* orders resolved — memos expunged; and Kibra withheld telephone numbers, so the live question is about ID numbers only.) Settles how section 1 and areas 2.3 and 2.15 should describe the prospects of an unredacted-forms request.
5. **Does s.39(1C)(c) oblige the IEBC to publish the prescribed fields unredacted?** *The Schedule question is resolved (CORRECTED: LN 72/2017 r.44 prescribes the ID/passport and telephone columns on Forms 34A, 34B and 34C).* The live question is whether "publish the polling result forms", read with the prescribed Schedule and the Kibra reasoning, requires the Commission to publish the identifiers, or whether IEBC Act s.27(6) and DPA s.51(1) let it minimise. *Okoiti* [2026] KEHC 13916 declined Art. 35 "manner of publication" prayers without reaching personal data. Decisive for the IEBC-as-controller scenario (areas 2.3, 2.8, 2.9).
6. **Draft s.47 notice (Sept 2026) on "political affiliation and trade union membership".** *Existence and draft status resolved (CONFIRMED; consultation to 25 Sept 2026).* Read the text and the exact cross-border limitation when odpc.go.ke is back; watch for a gazetted final notice. Settles whether area 2.4 needs P2 and whether redacted pages (name + party) become sensitive data for area 2.1.
7. ***Gachoka & 3 others v CS National Treasury & 11 others* [2026] KEHC 13493 (15 Sept 2026).** *The Data Protection (Amendment) Bill 2025 point is resolved (CORRECTED: no Bill before Parliament).* Read the court's treatment of reg. 26 and of the argument that Safaricom hosts IEBC systems including the results transmission network: it may be the first judicial reading of reg. 26's reach and of what "in Kenya" means for a hosted service (area 2.1). *Resolved (second pass):* the court did not construe reg. 26 (the description is the respondents' submission); it decided on Art. 238.
8. **IEBC results systems as critical information infrastructure (CMCA s.9 gazette notices).** STILL UNVERIFIED (second pass: no designation notice in Kenya Law's index, Gazette included; negative search only). If gazetted, LN 44 of 2024 reg. 28 imposes a second localisation rule with committee approval for any offshore location (areas 2.1, 2.18).
9. **IEBC's ODPC registration and the operator's own registration duty.** The National Assembly's 15 October 2026 resolution calling on the IEBC "to expedite its registration" (CONFIRMED) implies it is not registered; confirm on the ODPC register when reachable. Apply LN 265 reg. 13 to the operator's turnover and headcount, and the Third Schedule (item 1 "canvassing political support" should not apply to publication, but confirm); check whether the reported AI Guidance requires registration of entities deploying AI on Kenyans' data (area 2.9).
10. **ODPC Guidance Notes: electoral (2021/2022), DPIA (28 Nov 2023), cross-border (Sept 2026), AI (draft July 2026), public sector (Nov 2025).** None could be read on either pass (site under maintenance). Confirm whether the electoral note says anything about result forms, agents or polling officials, and track the six-month update the National Assembly asked for on 15 October 2026 (CONFIRMED) (areas 2.3–2.6, 2.8).
11. **Election Offences Act 2016, ss.13(d)–(e) and 17 (now examined, B-verification §11).** No provision expressly addresses publishing an altered copy of a result form; confirm with counsel that a labelled redacted copy, with the original untouched and identified, is outside s.13(d) ("interferes with election material by … concealing or mutilating"), s.13(e) (supplying "election material … save on the authority of the Commission") and s.17, and outside CMCA s.22–23 (area 2.13, E1).
